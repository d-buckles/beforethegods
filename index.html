The Argument

What This Book Claims

For most of human history, religion did not involve gods. It involved powers — specific, local, material, and encountered rather than believed in. A spring, an ancestor, a grove, a season. These were addressed through gift and avoided through taboo, and the relationship was maintained by practice rather than affirmed by doctrine.

Named, personified deities with biographies and priesthoods are a comparatively late development, arriving with state formation, agricultural surplus, and writing. They did not replace the older stratum. They were layered over it — and underneath, in folk practice and domestic ritual, the older logic persisted, in some places into living memory.

That is the claim. Chapter II sets out how much weight the evidence can bear, and Chapter XVIII tests the framework against traditions it was not built from — where it strains. The reader is invited to hold both alongside everything in between.

Chapter I

Definition & the Problem of "Paganism"

Before we can speak about what ancient non-theistic pagans believed, we must grapple with the word itself — a term born in contempt, reshaped by scholarship, and still contested in the twenty-first century.

The country people were the last to keep the old rites, and so the old rites took their name.

On the origin of paganus

Etymology

Where the Word Comes From

The Latin word paganus had two overlapping meanings in classical Rome: "rural dweller" or "villager," and by extension "civilian" (as opposed to a soldier). Neither meaning had any inherent religious content. The Roman legions used paganus to contrast themselves with civilians who had not taken the military oath (sacramentum), and early Christians repurposed this military metaphor — the Christian was a soldier of Christ, enrolled in God's army; those outside the faith were civilians, pagani, who had not yet enlisted.

The first clearly religious use of the term dates to around 370 CE, in contexts where paganus explicitly means "non-Christian." The shift from "rural person" to "non-Christian" reflects a social reality: Christianity spread fastest through cities (civitates), while the countryside (pagus) retained older practices much longer. The word thus encodes both a geographical and a religious othering.

Primary Source

The historian Tertullian (c. 200 CE) and later Augustine of Hippo used paganus interchangeably with gentilis (gentile) and ethnicus (ethnic/national), all meaning "practitioner of traditional, non-Christian religion." The conflation of "rural," "civilian," and "non-Christian" into one term was complete by the late 4th century.

The Greek Parallel: Hellene

In the Greek-speaking Eastern Roman Empire, the equivalent term was Hellene — originally simply "Greek person" — which by the 4th century CE had been reloaded to mean "practitioner of traditional Greek polytheistic religion." The Emperor Julian (r.c. 361–363 CE), himself a committed polytheist attempting to reverse Christianization, deliberately reclaimed Hellene as a proud self-designation. His failed project is among the last major attempts to systematize traditional pagan religion before Christian hegemony became total in the Roman world.

Scholarly History

How Scholars Remade the Word

For most of the Christian Middle Ages and early modernity, "pagan" was a polemical term — something other people were, something to be converted or condemned. The scholarly rehabilitation of the word as a neutral category began in earnest in the nineteenth century, when comparative religion emerged as a discipline and European scholars began taking non-Christian traditions seriously as objects of intellectual study.

Max Müller and the Science of Religion (1860s–1890s)

Friedrich Max Müller (1823–1900), the German-British philologist, is often credited as the founder of comparative religion as a formal academic discipline. His 1856 lectures Comparative Mythology and later his editing of the monumental Sacred Books of the East series (50 volumes, 1879–1910) established that non-Christian, non-Jewish, non-Islamic religious texts deserved the same scholarly attention as the Bible.

Müller proposed naturism — the interpretation of natural phenomena (sun, storm, dawn) — as the origin of myth and religion. He famously described mythology as "a disease of language": the sun's nightly "death" became a story of a dying god. Though his specific theory has been largely abandoned, Müller's framework opened the comparative study of myth and established that ancient paganism was a coherent object of intellectual inquiry.

"He who knows one religion knows none."

— F. Max Müller, Introduction to the Science of Religion (1873)

E.B. Tylor and Primitive Culture (1871)

Edward Burnett Tylor's two-volume Primitive Culture (1871) is the foundational text of modern anthropology of religion. Tylor coined the word animism (from Latin anima, soul/breath) as his proposed minimum definition of religion: the belief in spiritual beings. He argued this belief arose from ancient humans' attempts to explain two puzzling phenomena — dreams (where the self seems to leave the body) and death (where the animating principle departs permanently).

Tylor was an evolutionist: he believed religion had developed through stages, from animism through polytheism to monotheism, with the latter representing intellectual progress. This evolutionary framing is now rejected — it smuggles in a value judgment that monotheism is "more advanced" — but Tylor's descriptive core (animism as the basic structure of archaic religion) remains foundational.

Key Text

E.B. Tylor, Primitive Culture: Researches into the Development of Mythology, Philosophy, Religion, Art, and Custom (1871, 2 vols.) — Still in print. Vol. I, Chapters XI–XIV cover animism in full. Essential primary source for any study of the anthropological foundations of pagan religion.

Andrew Lang's Critique and the High God Problem

Scottish folklorist Andrew Lang (1844–1912) challenged Tylor's evolutionary scheme by demonstrating that many tribal peoples worldwide held belief in a single high creator deity alongside or even prior to animism. Lang's The Making of Religion (1898) argued that monotheism and animism may be simultaneous strata rather than sequential stages, complicating any simple evolutionary model and suggesting that "primitive religion" was more complex than Tylor had allowed.

Émile Durkheim: Religion as Social Fact (1912)

Durkheim's The Elementary Forms of Religious Life (1912) shifted the question entirely. For Durkheim, religion was not primarily about beliefs (Tylor's concern) but about collective practice and social solidarity. The sacred was not a feature of the world but a projection of society itself — what a community marks as set-apart, collective, and binding is "sacred," regardless of what cosmological framework surrounds it.

This Durkheimian framework allows us to understand non-theistic pagan practice without reducing it to "failed science" (Tylor's implicit framing) — it was socially functional, community-organizing, and meaningful on its own terms.

"Religious representations are collective representations which express collective realities; the rites are a manner of acting which take rise in the midst of the assembled groups and which are destined to excite, maintain, or recreate certain mental states in these groups."

— Émile Durkheim, The Elementary Forms of Religious Life (1912), trans. Karen Fields

The Non-Theistic Question

What Does "Non-Theistic" Mean in This Context?

The philosophical distinction between theism, deism, pantheism, and non-theism is a product of European early-modern philosophy and was not available to ancient peoples as a self-description. When scholars use "non-theistic paganism," they mean something specific and precise: religious traditions organized around sacred power that was not yet (or never) conceptualized as a named, personified deity with agency, will, and biography.

Theism

Personified Deity

A god has a name, personality, history, intentions, and usually a relationship to humanity as creator, protector, or judge. Zeus, Odin, Yahweh — these are theistic figures.

Non-Theistic Power

Impersonal Sacred Force

The spring, the grove, the ancestor, the totem animal — these have power, agency, and presence but not a personal name, biography, or moral relationship with humanity as such.

The Ambiguous Zone

Proto-Theism

Many ancient figures sit between — the power of a spring half-personified as a "lady of the water" is neither purely impersonal nor a full theistic deity. This is the transitional zone.

The Test

How to Tell Them Apart

Ask what happens if the power is moved. A god travels with its people and keeps its name; a non-theistic power cannot be relocated, because it is the spring, the grove, or the grave. Portability marks the theistic turn.

The critical distinction is not between "spiritual" and "non-spiritual" but between sacred powers that are specific, local, impersonal, and addressed through direct material relationship (offerings, presence, behavior) versus sacred powers that have consolidated into persons with universal reach, mythology, and priesthoods. Ancient non-theistic paganism is firmly in the first category.

Modern Scholarship

Contemporary Frameworks

Ronald Hutton and the Difficulty of Pre-Christian Religion

Ronald Hutton, Professor of History at the University of Bristol and the foremost historian of British pre-Christian religion, has repeatedly emphasized how fragmentary our evidence is. In Pagan Britain (2013) and The Druids (2007), Hutton argues that we must resist projecting either later theistic paganism or modern Neopaganism backward onto prehistoric practice. What the archaeology shows is ritual behavior without decipherable theology — which is itself a finding, not a gap to be filled.

Graham Harvey and Neo-Animism

Anthropologist Graham Harvey's Animism: Respecting the Living World (2005) articulates what he calls the "new animism" — a revised understanding of animist traditions that replaces Tylor's patronizing "proto-science" framing with a serious engagement with what animist peoples themselves say they are doing. Harvey's animism is relational: the world is populated with persons (not all of them human) and the task of religion is to learn to behave appropriately toward them.

"Animism is not a belief that objects have souls. It is a recognition that the world is full of persons, only some of whom are human, and that life is always lived in relationship with others."

— Graham Harvey, Animism: Respecting the Living World (2005)

David Abram and the Ecological Dimension

Philosopher David Abram's The Spell of the Sensuous (1996) approaches animist and indigenous religion from a phenomenological angle, arguing that pre-literate peoples maintained a deep perceptual reciprocity with their environments that alphabetic literacy gradually severed. For Abram, animism is not a belief system but a mode of perceptual engagement with a world experienced as inherently communicative and responsive.

The Cognitive Science Approach

Since the 1990s, cognitive scientists of religion — Pascal Boyer, Harvey Whitehouse, Justin Barrett — have asked why animist beliefs are so universal and persistent across cultures. Their answer: the human brain is equipped with hyperactive agency detection (HADD — Hyperactive Agency Detection Device), which causes us to perceive intentional agency in ambiguous natural phenomena. This is not an error but an adaptive feature — false positives (assuming a rustling bush conceals a predator) are safer than false negatives. Animism, on this account, is a cognitive default, not a cultural invention.

Chapter II

The Archaeology of Belief

Every claim in this book rests on an inference from a physical object to a vanished state of mind. That inference is not free. This chapter sets out how it is made, what licenses it, and where it fails — so that the rest can be read with the right degree of trust.

We do not dig up belief. We dig up its residue, and argue about what fell.

On method

The Core Problem

From Potsherd to Worldview

Prehistoric religion presents a difficulty no other branch of religious studies faces in the same acute form. The scholar of Buddhism has sutras. The historian of Roman religion has Ovid, inscriptions, and a calendar. The archaeologist of the Upper Paleolithic has a pierced shell, a scatter of ochre, and a bear skull on a rock ledge.

The gap between those objects and any statement about belief is enormous, and it cannot be closed by evidence alone. It is closed — when it is closed at all — by argument. The honest question is not whether inference is involved but whether a particular inference is disciplined.

Consider a concrete case. At Shanidar Cave in Iraq, a Neanderthal burial excavated by Ralph Solecki in 1960 contained unusual concentrations of pollen. Solecki proposed the "Flower Burial": the dead had been laid on a bed of blossoms, implying grief, ceremony, and perhaps belief in an afterlife. It became one of the most cited findings in the study of prehistoric religion.

It has also been substantially undermined. Jeffrey Sommer argued in 1999 that the pollen was likely introduced by Meriones persicus, the Persian jird — a burrowing rodent that stores flower heads in its nests. The pollen clusters matched rodent caching behavior better than deliberate deposition. More recent work at the site has partially revived the case for intentional burial on other grounds, but the flowers themselves are no longer good evidence for anything.

The Shanidar flowers are a cautionary tale in miniature: a striking interpretation, widely repeated, resting on a taphonomic process nobody had ruled out.

— On the reassessment of Shanidar IV

The lesson generalizes. Before any find can support a religious interpretation, the mundane explanations must be eliminated — and the mundane explanations are usually more numerous than they first appear.

The Inference Chain

Four Steps, Four Places to Fail

Any argument from material remains to religious belief passes through four stages. Each is a genuine inference, and an error at any stage invalidates everything downstream.

Step 1

Is the pattern real?

Distinguishing deliberate human arrangement from natural process — water sorting, animal disturbance, root action, frost heave. Taphonomy governs this step, and it defeats more interpretations than any other.

Step 2

Is it non-functional?

A pit may be a shrine or a rubbish dump. Ritual interpretation requires showing that practical explanations do not account for what is observed — deposition too costly, too orderly, or too consistent for refuse.

Step 3

Is it symbolic?

Non-functional is not yet religious. Play, aesthetics, social display, and identity marking are all non-functional. Symbolic interpretation needs patterned repetition that tracks something beyond the individual act.

Step 4

What did it mean?

The weakest step by a wide margin. Content — what was believed, which powers were addressed, what the participants thought was happening — is largely unrecoverable without text. Most honest accounts stop before this point.

This book makes claims at step three with reasonable confidence, and at step four only where written or ethnographic sources supply independent support. Where a chapter asserts more than that, it should be read as a proposal rather than a finding.

The Ritual Fallacy

"Ritual" as the Residual Category

There is a standing joke in archaeology that anything an excavator cannot explain gets labeled ritual. The joke identifies a real methodological vice. When function is obscure, "ritual" becomes the default — not because evidence points there, but because the category is elastic enough to absorb anything.

The critique was pressed most forcefully in processual archaeology from the 1960s onward. Lewis Binford and colleagues argued that archaeology should confine itself to what material evidence can actually sustain — subsistence, technology, exchange, demography — and treat cognitive and religious claims as beyond reach. On this view the entire subject of this book is a category error.

The post-processual response, developed by Ian Hodder and others from the 1980s, held that excluding meaning does not produce objectivity; it produces a description of past peoples as though they were not people. Symbolic behavior is not an optional overlay on subsistence but part of how subsistence itself was organized. The question is not whether to interpret but how to do it accountably.

The working compromise most field archaeologists now adopt: ritual interpretation requires positive evidence, not merely the absence of a functional account. Specifically, one looks for costly signaling (labor or material sacrificed beyond utility), formalized repetition (the same act performed the same way across generations), spatial segregation (activity confined to bounded places), and structured deposition (material placed according to a pattern rather than discarded).

The Methodological Debate

Renfrew's Criteria

What Ritual Looks Like in the Ground

Colin Renfrew's The Archaeology of Cult (1985), based on excavation at the sanctuary of Phylakopi on Melos, proposed the first explicit checklist for identifying ritual archaeologically. His criteria remain the standard starting point. Ritual activity tends to leave some combination of:

Attention

Focusing Devices

Architecture or objects that direct attention to one location — an altar, a hearth, a pillar, a niche. The space is organized around something.

Boundary

Liminal Zones

Marked thresholds between ordinary and special space: enclosure ditches, doorways, cave mouths, water margins. Crossing is controlled.

Presence

Images or Symbols

Representations, whether figurative or abstract, that recur in the same context — suggesting an addressee, or the concept being invoked.

Offering

Participation & Deposition

Material given up rather than used: food remains, broken valuables, burned goods. The clearest signature, because the cost is visible.

No single criterion suffices. A hearth alone is cooking; a hearth inside an enclosure, containing repeatedly deposited unburned valuables, in a location with no domestic debris, is something else. Confidence comes from convergence, not from any one feature.

Ethnographic Analogy

Borrowing from the Living

Where material evidence runs out, archaeologists frequently reach for ethnography: if contemporary or recently documented peoples do X for reason Y, perhaps prehistoric people deposited similar material for similar reasons. Much of what this book says about animism and shamanism depends on such analogy.

The move is indispensable and genuinely dangerous. Its dangers are worth naming plainly:

The living are not the past. Contemporary hunter-gatherers are not Paleolithic survivals; they have their own histories, and most have been shaped by centuries of contact, displacement, and trade. Treating them as time capsules is both bad method and a form of condescension.

Analogy selects for what it seeks. If one begins expecting shamanism, the ethnographic record is vast enough to supply a matching parallel for nearly any artifact. Without a rule constraining which comparisons are admissible, the method can confirm anything.

Universals may be shallow. Structural similarities across cultures may reflect a common feature of human cognition — or the categories that Western observers brought with them. Alice Kehoe's critique of "shamanism" as a scholarly construct bears directly here.

The disciplined version, sometimes called relational analogy, requires that the comparison rest on a demonstrable connection: similar ecology, similar technology, similar social scale, or documented historical continuity. General resemblance is not enough.

What Survives

The Preservation Filter

A final distortion is imposed not by method but by chemistry. What we recover is not a sample of what existed; it is what happened to survive, and survival is powerfully biased.

Survives well

Stone, bone, fired clay, gold

Durable across millennia in most conditions. Hence a record dominated by lithics and ceramics — and a discipline whose periods are named after them.

Survives rarely

Wood, textile, leather, basketry

Preserved only in waterlogged, frozen, or arid conditions. The bog bodies and the Alpine ice finds exist because of local chemistry, not because such practices were confined to bogs and glaciers.

Never survives

Song, speech, gesture, dance, story

The greater part of any religion. Every oral tradition, every prayer, every name of every power is simply gone. What remains is the physical residue of practices whose meaning lived in words.

This filter has a specific consequence for the argument of this book. The absence of evidence for named deities in the Paleolithic is not strong evidence of absence — names do not fossilize. The positive case for a non-theistic reading rests instead on what is present: the pattern of place-bound, materially mediated, locally specific practice, sustained over tens of thousands of years, and on the observable transition to portable, named, personified cult in the literate periods that follow.

That is a real argument, and a defeasible one. Read the chapters that follow with the four-step chain in mind, and it should be possible to see at each point which step a given claim depends on.

Animism

The foundational religious intuition of humanity: that the world is not a collection of inert objects but a community of subjects — alive, responsive, and deserving of relationship.

The world is full of persons, only some of whom are human.

Graham Harvey
c. 100,000
Years BCE — earliest confirmed burial ritual implying spirit belief (Skhul Cave, Israel)

The Core Claim

What Animism Actually Asserts

Animism is not primitive confusion. It is not the mistake of believing that rocks and rivers are alive the same way mammals are alive. The animist claim is more precise and more philosophically interesting: that the world contains multiple kinds of subjectivity, multiple kinds of interiority and agency, and that human beings are not uniquely endowed with soul or consciousness. A river has its own mode of being-alive; an ancestor has a different mode; a stone has another still. None of these is identical to human subjectivity, but all are forms of presence that require acknowledgment.

This is the position articulated not only by Tylor in 1871 but increasingly by contemporary philosophers working within phenomenology (Merleau-Ponty, Abram), process philosophy (Whitehead, Griffin), and even analytic philosophy of mind, where panpsychism is now a seriously debated academic position. The animist intuition turns out to be robust.

Critically, animism is not a doctrine that must be believed. It is closer to a stance — a default orientation toward the world in which responsiveness is expected rather than surprising. The animist does not conclude that the river is a person after weighing evidence. The animist meets the river already expecting a counterpart.

Seeing a stone as a center of perception is not a claim about the stone's nervous system. It is a claim about what kind of world this is — one in which attention is owed in many directions, not only toward other humans.

— After David Abram, The Spell of the Sensuous (1996)

Tylor's Framework

The Victorian Founding of Animism Studies

The Two Source Problems

Tylor proposed that animism arose as ancient humans tried to explain two puzzling experiences. The first was the dream: during sleep, the self seems to leave the body and inhabit another realm, encountering the dead and the distant. The simplest explanation was that something detachable — a soul — temporarily departed. The second was death: the body remains but the animating principle vanishes. These experiences led to the conviction that living beings possessed two components — body and soul — and that the soul could survive the body.

From this base, Tylor argued, ancient people extended the logic outward. If humans have souls, why not animals? Why not plants? Why not rivers and mountains, which behave in ways that seem purposive — flooding, drying, growing, withholding? The animating principle, anima, was gradually distributed across the whole of nature.

Tylor's account has an obvious weakness, which his critics identified early: it makes animism a kind of failed physics, a mistaken theory that better information would correct. This mischaracterizes what animist peoples actually say and do. But the descriptive core — that soul-belief is the minimum structure of archaic religion — has survived a century and a half of criticism largely intact.

Tylor's Spirit Typology

Tylor distinguished several categories of spiritual entity that recur across animist traditions:

Type 1

The Soul (Anima)

The animating principle of a living being — what makes it alive, what departs at death, and what may persist afterward. Every person, animal, and plant may have one.

Type 2

The Ghost

The soul of a dead person who remains near the living — especially the recently dead. Requires propitiation, respect, and often feeding to prevent harmful behavior.

Type 3

Nature Spirits

The inherent spiritual power of a natural feature — a spring, forest, mountain, storm. Not a deity; not a ghost. A localized, non-personal presence with power.

Type 4

The Free Spirit

A disembodied entity not attached to any specific person, animal, or place — a power that moves through the world and can be encountered, bargained with, or feared.

Key Text

E.B. Tylor, Primitive Culture (1871, 2 vols.) — Volume I, Chapters XI–XIV contain the full statement of the animism thesis. Still in print and still the necessary starting point.

The New Animism

Beyond Tylor: The Relational Turn

Tylor's animism, for all its insight, was embedded in a colonial, evolutionary framework that treated animist peoples as "primitive" and their beliefs as prescientific. From the 1980s onward, anthropologists working closely with indigenous communities developed a radically different account — sometimes called the new animism or relational animism.

Nurit Bird-David and Relational Epistemology

Nurit Bird-David's 1999 article "'Animism' Revisited: Personhood, Environment, and Relational Epistemology" (Current Anthropology 40:S1) is the founding text of this turn. Working with the Nayaka people of South India, Bird-David argued that what Tylor called animism is not a belief about souls but an epistemological practice — a way of knowing the world through attending to the relationships and responses it offers.

For the Nayaka, to call something a person (devaru) is not to make a metaphysical claim about its internal states. It is to say: I engage with this in a relationship; it responds to me; we hold a mutual attentiveness. Animism, on this account, is a relational epistemology rather than a faulty ontology — a claim about how knowing works, not a mistaken inventory of what exists.

Animism can be understood as a relational ontology in which human and non-human are not categorically divided; relationships run between differently positioned beings within one shared moral and social field.

— After Nurit Bird-David, Current Anthropology 40:S1 (1999)

Graham Harvey's Living World

Harvey's formulation, drawing on Bird-David and on fieldwork with indigenous communities worldwide, defines animism simply: the world is full of persons, only some of whom are human, and life is always lived in relationship with others. The task of animist religion is learning what each kind of person requires — how to greet, how to take, how to give, how to refuse — and doing so with appropriate ceremony.

This framework has transformed how scholars read archaeological and historical evidence from ancient Europe and the Near East. The question shifts from "did these people believe in gods?" to "what persons did they recognize, and how did they conduct themselves toward them?" The second question is answerable from material remains in a way the first is not.

David Abram and the Perceptual Account

Philosopher David Abram's The Spell of the Sensuous (1996) approaches animism phenomenologically, arguing that pre-literate peoples maintained a perceptual reciprocity with their environments that alphabetic literacy gradually severed. For Abram, animism is not a belief system at all but a mode of perceptual engagement with a world experienced as communicative. Writing, he argues, transferred the experience of encountering meaningful signs from the living landscape to the page.

Cognitive Science

Why Animism Is Universal

HADD — Hyperactive Agency Detection

Cognitive scientists Pascal Boyer (Religion Explained, 2001) and Justin Barrett (Why Would Anyone Believe in God?, 2004) proposed that the human agency detection system is systematically biased toward false positives. We are built to ask "is something there?" before "is nothing there?" — because missing a real predator is fatal, while assuming a false one costs almost nothing.

This HADD system, adaptive in a world of real predators, generates the intuition that natural events have agents behind them — that the storm is doing something, that the river wants something, that the ancestor is watching. Animism, on this view, is a cognitive default that requires sustained cultural intervention to override — and even then never fully disappears. Adults in thoroughly secular societies still apologize to furniture they bump into.

Theory of Mind Running Wide

Related to HADD is Theory of Mind — the capacity to attribute beliefs, desires, and intentions to others. It evolved for reading other humans, but it runs automatically on any input displaying the right cues: self-initiated movement, responsiveness, apparent purpose. Rivers, fire, and wind all display these cues under the right conditions. Animism may simply be Theory of Mind operating without the cultural filter that restricts person-attribution to biological categories.

Archaeological Evidence

Animism in the Deep Record

c. 300,000 BCE

Schöningen Spears, Germany

Finely balanced throwing spears implying planning, teaching, and coordinated hunting. Not religion, but evidence of the cognitive complexity that animist thought presupposes.

c. 100,000 BCE

Skhul & Qafzeh Burials, Israel

Anatomically modern humans buried with ochre and pierced shell. The deliberate, ceremonial treatment of the dead — the oldest confirmed ritual behavior — implies belief in the person's continued significance after death.

c. 77,000 BCE

Blombos Cave Ochre, South Africa

Ochre blocks engraved with deliberate geometric patterns. Symbolic thought — encoding meaning in marks — is a precondition for animist religious expression, and this is its earliest secure evidence.

c. 40,000 BCE

Lion-Man of Hohlenstein-Stadel, Germany

A 40 cm mammoth-ivory figure fusing human body and lion head. The oldest known therianthrope. It requires a mind in which human and animal identity can merge — the signature of animist and shamanic cognition. No theistic reading is needed.

c. 36,000 BCE

Chauvet Cave, France

The oldest securely dated cave paintings: over 400 animal images — lions, rhinos, bears, horses. Current consensus favors trance-vision recording over hunting magic: contact with animal spirits achieved in the liminal, resonant dark.

c. 34,000 BCE

Sunghir Burials, Russia

Two children interred head-to-head with thousands of mammoth-ivory beads, fox-tooth ornaments, and straightened mammoth-tusk spears. The labor invested in equipping the dead is among the strongest indicators of belief in continued existence.

c. 17,000 BCE

Lascaux, France

The Shaft Scene — a bird-headed figure, a disemboweled bison, a bird-topped staff — is among the most debated images in prehistoric art. The dominant reading is a practitioner in trance among the animal powers.

The Problem of Silence

All interpretation of Paleolithic religion is inference from material remains. We have objects, pigments, arrangements, and architecture. We do not have prayers, explanations, or names. Scholars including Steven Mithen (The Prehistory of the Mind, 1996) have argued forcefully for restraint: much that looks like religion may be craft practice, social signaling, or something for which we hold no modern category.

But restraint cuts both ways. The patterns — burial with ochre, therianthropic figures, animal imagery placed in acoustically active deep chambers — are systematic, repeated, and cross-culturally consistent. They reflect a shared behavioral repertoire compatible with animist belief, even where the specific cosmology that organized it is permanently beyond recovery.

Chapter III

Ancestor Cult & Manism

In the oldest strata of human religion, the dead are not gone. They change their mode of presence — from embodied to incorporeal — and continue to participate in the life of family, land, and community. This is the religion of ancestors.

The dead do not leave. They change address.

On ancestor cult

The Theory

Herbert Spencer and the Ancestor Hypothesis

The philosopher Herbert Spencer (1820–1903), in his Principles of Sociology (1876), made the provocative argument that all religion derives from ancestor veneration. His claim: the first "supernatural beings" were not sky gods or nature spirits but the recent dead — kin whose power, personality, and social weight did not simply evaporate when they died. Feeding, consulting, and propitiating the dead was the first religious act, and every later form of religion — including theism — is an elaboration of this original impulse.

Spencer's strong thesis has not survived intact. We now know from comparative evidence that nature spirits and shamanic belief often coexist with or even precede elaborate ancestor cults. But his core observation — that ancestor veneration is among the most ancient, persistent, and cross-culturally universal forms of religious practice — is amply confirmed by the archaeological and ethnographic record.

"Each tribe has, in the ghost of its great man, an object of awe and propitiation whose nature has been defined by personal experience — a being of known character, known powers, known caprices."

— Herbert Spencer, Principles of Sociology, Vol. I (1876)

What Manism Is (and Isn't)

Manism (from Latin manes, "the shades of the dead") refers specifically to the cult of the recently and remotely dead as sources of spiritual power and social authority. It is distinct from full deity worship in several important ways:

Manism

The Dead as Kin

Ancestors are addressed as family members whose obligations and relationships continue after death. The relationship is one of kinship, not worship. You owe them respect because they are yours.

Theism

Gods as Sovereigns

A god is typically addressed as a sovereign, creator, or universal power — not as a family member. The relationship is of a subject to a ruler, a creature to its creator.

The Continuum

Hero Cult

Between ancestor and god lies the hero — a powerful ancestor whose influence has expanded beyond their biological kin group to become a regional or communal power. Greek hero cult is the clearest historical example.

The Boundary

The Forgotten Dead

Ancestor status is not automatic. The dead who receive no offerings, or whose descendants die out, lapse into the anonymous mass of the departed — still powerful, but no longer kin. Much ritual exists to prevent this lapse.

Skull Cults

Neolithic Skull Veneration: Keeping the Dead Present

Among the most extraordinary archaeological evidence for ancestor cult is the practice of skull curation — the deliberate removal, modification, and continued use of human skulls after death. This practice appears across multiple independent Neolithic and Mesolithic cultures and represents perhaps the most direct possible evidence for belief in the continued presence and power of the dead.

Ain Ghazal and Jericho (Jordan / Palestine, c. 7,000–6,000 BCE)

At Pre-Pottery Neolithic B (PPNB) sites in the Levant, particularly at Jericho and Ain Ghazal, archaeologists discovered a remarkable practice: skulls were separated from the skeleton after initial burial, the facial features were modeled in plaster (sometimes with shell eyes inserted to restore the gaze), and the resulting plastered skulls were kept in domestic or communal spaces — sometimes for generations.

These are not trophies. The care taken to recreate individual facial features, the installation of gaze-restoring eyes, the placement in living areas rather than graves — all point to the same interpretation: the dead ancestor was still present, still a member of the household, still watching. The skull was the continued physical interface with a person whose social existence had not terminated.

Archaeological Source

Garfinkel, Y. (1994). "Ritual Burial of Cultic Objects: The Earliest Evidence." Cambridge Archaeological Journal 4(2): 159–188. See also: Kuijt, I. (2008). "The Regeneration of Life: Neolithic Structures of Symbolic Remembering and Forgetting." Current Anthropology 49(2): 171–197.

The Mesolithic of Europe

Skull curation is not limited to the Near East. At Ofnet Cave in Bavaria, Germany, two "nests" of skulls were found, dating to around 6500 BCE — 38 skulls, many bearing ochre staining and ornaments. Some show perimortem trauma (violent death); others show post-mortem curation. The arrangement — skulls facing west, carefully placed — is clearly ceremonial.

Similar finds appear at Aveline's Hole and other Mesolithic sites in Britain. The pattern is consistent: selected skulls are removed from their skeletons, treated with ochre, and placed in meaningful configurations. The dead — or at least their most person-representing fragment — are not buried and forgotten but retained as participants.

Neolithic Tombs

Houses for the Dead: Megalithic Tomb Architecture

The most architecturally ambitious expression of Neolithic ancestor religion is the construction of collective megalithic tombs across Atlantic Europe (c. 4,000–2,000 BCE). These are not simply graves. They are permanent structures built for ongoing use — the dead are placed inside, but the living return repeatedly to add new dead, to perform ceremonies, and to commune with the accumulated ancestral power within.

Newgrange, Ireland (c. 3,200 BCE)

The passage tomb at Newgrange in the Boyne Valley is one of the best-preserved Neolithic structures in the world — older than Stonehenge and the Egyptian pyramids. Its defining feature is the roof-box: a narrow aperture above the entrance passage that allows a shaft of light to penetrate the central chamber only during the winter solstice sunrise, illuminating the interior for approximately seventeen minutes.

This is not accident. It required precise astronomical knowledge and centuries of accumulated observation to achieve. The design purpose is clear: at the moment of the year's greatest darkness — the solstice — the ancestors housed in the chamber are touched by light. The living gathered outside to witness this event. It is a ceremony of renewal in which the dead and the turning of the year are directly connected. No named deity is required or implied — the power is in the bones, the stone, and the returning sun.

Maeshowe, Orkney (c. 2,800 BCE)

Maeshowe is architecturally similar to Newgrange: a passage tomb aligned to the winter solstice sunset, such that the setting sun illuminates the rear wall of the chamber. Its stonework is among the finest of any Neolithic structure in Europe. The Orkney complex — including the Ring of Brodgar and Stones of Stenness nearby — suggests a regional ceremonial landscape organized around the interplay between the living, the dead, and seasonal astronomical cycles.

The Hypogeum of Hal Saflieni, Malta (c. 3,600–2,500 BCE)

A subterranean complex of chambers cut directly into the rock — the world's only known prehistoric underground temple. The remains of approximately 7,000 individuals were found within, along with extraordinary artifacts. Certain chambers have acoustic properties that amplify resonant male voices dramatically, suggesting deliberate acoustic engineering for ritual purposes — chant or ceremonial speech designed to invoke the presence of the dead housed in the rock.

The Bones Themselves: Commingling and Curation

Inside most megalithic tombs, the bones of the dead are not kept separate and individual. They are commingled — mixed together, sometimes sorted by type (long bones in one area, skulls in another), rearranged over time as new burials were added. This treatment indicates that individual identity was not the point. The tomb was a vessel for collective ancestral power — the accumulated spiritual weight of all those who had ever belonged to this community. The individual dissolved into the ancestral whole.

"The megalithic tomb was not a place of deposition but a place of power — a threshold where the accumulated weight of the community's dead could be engaged by the living at critical moments: solstices, planting, harvest, crisis."

— Paraphrase of Ronald Hutton, Pagan Britain (2013)

The Roman Household

Lares, Penates, and the Di Manes: Ancestor Cult in Historical Rome

Even in historical, literate, officially polytheistic Rome, the most intimate religious practice remained thoroughly non-theistic in structure: the domestic cult of the household dead. This pre-Olympian stratum of Roman religion persisted throughout the republic and empire, practiced daily in every Roman home regardless of official state religion.

The Lares

The Lares (singular Lar) were the spirits of the household — originally the ancestral dead of the family, in some interpretations the spirit of the land on which the home was built. Every Roman home had a lararium — a household shrine — where small statues of the Lares were kept. Daily offerings of food, incense, and garlands were made at the lararium. The Lares were present at every significant family event: birth, coming of age, marriage, death.

The Lares were not gods. They were not universal, not powerful beyond the household, not part of any mythology. They were simply the family's own dead, concentrated and retained in the home as protective presences. Neglecting them was dangerous — they might become Larvae (malevolent shades) — but honoring them was more like family obligation than religious worship.

The Penates

The Penates (from penus, the inner storeroom) were the spirits of the household's food supply and economic wellbeing. Where the Lares protected the family as a social body, the Penates protected its material sustenance. Meals began with an offering to the Penates — a portion of food thrown into the hearth fire.

The Di Manes

The Di Manes ("the divine shades") were the collective dead of Rome — all the ancestral spirits of all Roman families, understood as a collective underworld power. During the festival of Parentalia (February 13–21) and the Lemuria (May 9, 11, 13), the boundary between the living and the dead thinned. During Lemuria especially, the potentially hostile dead (lemures) had to be expelled from the home by the paterfamilias through a midnight ritual of spitting black beans.

These festivals and practices have no connection to the Olympian pantheon. They are pre-Roman in origin, clearly descended from a much older stratum of Italian ancestor religion that predates the influence of Greek mythology on Roman culture.

Global Parallels

Ancestor Cult Across Cultures

Ancestor veneration is among the most cross-culturally persistent forms of religious practice in human history. Its presence across unconnected cultures argues for deep psychological and social roots, not cultural diffusion.

East Asia

Chinese Ancestral Religion

The oldest stratum of Chinese religion — pre-dating Confucianism and Taoism — centers on ancestral tablets (spirit tablets kept in the home), ancestral halls (communal shrines for clan dead), and regular offerings of food, incense, and paper goods burned to transmit them to the dead. Scholars date its origins to at least the Shang Dynasty (c.c. 1,600–1,046 BCE) and plausibly much earlier.

Sub-Saharan Africa

African Ancestral Religion

Across diverse African cultures from the Yoruba to the Zulu, the ancestors (egungun, amadlozi) are the primary spiritual authorities in daily life — consulted for major decisions, propitiated during illness or crisis, and honored at family and communal ceremonies. Ancestor religion is the deep structure beneath many West and Southern African spiritual traditions.

Mesoamerica

Day of the Dead

The Aztec festival of Miccailhuitl — the feast of the dead — long predates Spanish contact. The modern Mexican Día de los Muertos is a syncretic survival of an ancient Mesoamerican ancestor festival in which the dead were believed to return annually to share a meal with the living. The marigold-strewn ofrenda altar is a direct descendant of Aztec ancestor veneration.

Japan

Obon and Butsudan

The Japanese Obon festival (bon-odori) is an annual ancestor festival during which the dead are believed to return to visit their families. The household butsudan — a cabinet containing ancestral memorial tablets — is the center of daily non-Buddhist ancestor practice in millions of Japanese homes. This stratum predates and runs parallel to Buddhist influence.

Chapter V

Death, Burial & the Treatment of the Body

Funerary practice is the largest and richest body of evidence we possess for prehistoric religion — because burial is the one ritual that reliably leaves a deposit, and because what a community does with its dead reveals what it believes has happened to them.

Show me the manner in which a nation cares for its dead and I will measure the sympathies of its people.

Attributed to W.E. Gladstone
120,000
Years BCE — approximate date of the earliest deliberate burials at Qafzeh and Skhul

Why Burial Matters

The Evidence That Survives

Almost everything else a prehistoric community did religiously has vanished. Songs, prayers, processions, and the words spoken over a fire leave nothing. But a grave is a deliberate, durable, structured deposit — and the choices made in it were meaningful to the people who made them.

This creates a systematic bias worth stating at the outset: because burial dominates the surviving record, prehistoric religion can appear more death-focused than it was. A community that spent most of its ritual energy on seasonal renewal and hunting success will still be known to us chiefly through its graves.

With that caveat, the funerary record is extraordinarily informative. Body position, orientation, grave goods, the treatment of bones after decomposition, the location of the grave relative to the settlement — each is a decision, and decisions repeated across generations reveal structure.

The Methods

Six Ways to Treat a Body

The variety of funerary treatment in prehistory is wider than modern practice suggests. Each method implies a different understanding of what the corpse is and what must happen to it.

Method 1

Inhumation

Burial of the intact body. The most common and most archaeologically visible. Position (extended, flexed, crouched) and orientation are usually highly consistent within a culture — and consistency is the signature of rule.

Method 2

Cremation

Burning to reduce the body to bone fragments. Requires enormous fuel — a substantial cost, and therefore a deliberate choice. Often paired with urn burial. Implies transformation rather than preservation.

Method 3

Excarnation

Exposure of the body until the flesh is gone, then collection of the bones. Leaves distinctive weathering and gnaw marks. Separates the messy process of decay from the permanent, clean, curatable skeleton.

Method 4

Secondary Burial

A two-stage rite: temporary deposition, then later retrieval and permanent placement. The interval often corresponds to the mourning period — the dead are relocated once they have finished becoming dead.

Method 5

Water Deposition

Placement in bog, river, or lake. The northern European bog bodies are the best-known instances. Water margins are liminal — neither land nor open water — and receive both bodies and votive goods.

Method 6

Curation

Retention of the body or parts of it among the living: plastered skulls, ancestral bone bundles, relics kept in houses. The dead are not disposed of at all but relocated into domestic space.

Method 7

Sky Burial

Deliberate exposure for consumption by birds. Inferred at Çatalhöyük and Göbekli Tepe from vulture iconography paired with defleshed bone; documented historically in Tibet and Zoroastrian Iran.

Method 8

Mortuary Cannibalism

Consumption of the dead by the community. Attested at Gough's Cave in Somerset (c. 12,700 BCE), where skulls were fashioned into cups and long bones show butchery and human toothmarks. Endocannibalism is documented ethnographically as an act of respect, not desecration.

The Deep Record

The Emergence of Burial

c. 430,000 BCE

Sima de los Huesos, Spain

At least 28 individuals accumulated at the bottom of a 13-metre shaft, together with a single finely worked quartzite handaxe nicknamed Excalibur. Whether this is deliberate mortuary deposition or a natural trap remains disputed — but the handaxe, out of place among the bones, is hard to explain functionally.

c. 120,000 BCE

Qafzeh & Skhul, Israel

The earliest securely deliberate burials. At Qafzeh, a young individual interred with deer antlers placed on the chest; ochre use is associated with the site. Body position is arranged, not collapsed — the defining marker of intent.

c. 60,000 BCE

Shanidar, Iraq

Neanderthal burials including Shanidar 1, an individual who survived severe injuries — a withered arm, blindness in one eye, healed fractures — for years, implying sustained group care. The famous flower-pollen interpretation is now largely discounted, but the burials themselves remain significant.

c. 34,000 BCE

Sunghir, Russia

The most lavish Paleolithic burials known. Two children interred head to head with roughly 10,000 mammoth-ivory beads, fox-tooth belts, and straightened mammoth-tusk spears. Estimates put bead manufacture alone at thousands of hours of labor — expended entirely on the dead.

c. 12,700 BCE

Gough's Cave, England

Three skull-cups, carefully shaped by removing the facial bones and trimming the cranial vault to a bowl. Associated bone shows cut marks and human tooth impressions. The precision of the shaping argues for ritual rather than nutritional cannibalism.

c. 9,000 BCE

Jericho & 'Ain Ghazal, Levant

Plastered skulls: crania removed after decomposition, facial features rebuilt in lime plaster, eyes inlaid with shell. Kept and displayed in domestic space. The most explicit evidence anywhere for the dead as continuing household presences.

c. 7,000 BCE

Çatalhöyük, Anatolia

Burial beneath house floors, frequently under sleeping platforms. Certain houses accumulated dozens of interments across generations while neighbouring houses held none — suggesting particular dwellings served as ancestral foci for a wider group.

c. 3,200 BCE

Newgrange & the Passage Tombs

Collective deposition of cremated and unburnt remains in monumental stone chambers, aligned to solstice sunrise. Individual identity dissolves into commingled ancestral mass — the tomb is a reservoir of collective power, not a personal memorial.

c. 400 BCE

Tollund Man, Denmark

An Iron Age man hanged and placed in a bog, the noose still around his neck, his face composed. His last meal — a gruel of barley and seeds — survived in his gut. Deliberate killing followed by careful, almost tender deposition.

Reading the Grave

What Choices Reveal

Position and Orientation

The flexed or crouched position — knees drawn to the chest — is the most common prehistoric burial posture across Eurasia. Two readings compete and are not mutually exclusive: the fetal analogy, implying rebirth from the earth, and simple practicality, since a flexed body requires a smaller grave. Where the position is combined with consistent orientation, the ritual reading strengthens.

Orientation is often rigorously consistent within a culture: heads to the east, or toward a particular landmark, or aligned with the midwinter sunrise. Such consistency across hundreds of graves is not accidental, and it implies a shared cosmology in which direction carries meaning — a route the dead are expected to take.

Grave Goods

Objects placed with the dead admit several interpretations, and distinguishing them is rarely straightforward. Goods may equip the dead for a journey or a continued existence; they may express the survivors' status through conspicuous destruction of wealth; they may be items too contaminated by association with the deceased for the living to reuse.

The Sunghir burials illustrate the difficulty. Ten thousand ivory beads on two children represent a staggering investment. Because the children were too young to have earned status, the burials are usually read as evidence of ascribed rather than achieved rank — inherited social position. That is a claim about social structure derived entirely from mortuary evidence, and it shows both the power and the fragility of the inference.

Who Was Buried

In most prehistoric societies, formal burial was not universal. At many sites the number of recovered graves falls far below the population that must have lived and died there. The missing majority were disposed of in ways that leave no trace — exposure, river deposition, scattering.

This means burial was frequently a marked category: something done for particular people, for particular reasons. Identifying who qualified — by age, sex, lineage, manner of death, or role — is one of the most productive questions in mortuary archaeology, and one of the hardest to answer.

The Dangerous Dead

Deviant Burial and the Fear of Return

Alongside normative practice, the record contains burials that deliberately depart from it: bodies prone rather than supine, decapitated with the head placed between the legs, weighted with stones, pinned with stakes, buried at settlement margins or crossroads rather than in the cemetery.

These are usually interpreted as measures against the returning dead. The concern is not the soul's welfare but the community's safety — a category of dead considered dangerous and requiring physical restraint. The practice is documented from the Neolithic through the medieval period across Europe, and the medieval Slavic examples with iron sickles across the throat are only the most literal expression of a very old anxiety.

Deviant burial matters for the argument of this book because it reveals a belief structure with no theistic component whatsoever. There is no god judging the dead and no afterlife of reward or punishment. There is a corpse that may not stay put, and a set of physical techniques for ensuring that it does. This is chthonic and animist logic in its most practical form.

The stake through the body is not theology. It is engineering — applied to a problem the community regarded as entirely real.

— On deviant burial practice

Interpretation

Continuing Bonds, Not Departure

Taken together, the funerary record supports a specific reading. Across most of prehistory, the dead were not sent away. They were relocated — into the floor, into the tomb, into the bog, into a plastered skull on a shelf. The relationship continued in altered form, and it required maintenance.

This differs sharply from the salvation religions that follow, in which death initiates a journey to a separate destination and the living's obligation is discharged at the funeral. In the older pattern, obligation is ongoing and reciprocal: the dead are fed, consulted, and honoured; in return they protect, advise, and underwrite the fertility of the land they now inhabit.

Modern grief psychology arrived independently at a strikingly similar model. Continuing bonds theory, articulated by Klass, Silverman, and Nickman in 1996, argued against the then-dominant view that healthy mourning requires severing attachment to the deceased. Sustained relationship with the dead, they found, is normal and adaptive across cultures. The prehistoric evidence suggests it is also very old.

Chapter IV

Nature Religion & Sacred Geography

In the oldest human religion, the sacred is not above the world — it is in the world. Specific places, specific waters, specific rocks concentrate a power that is indigenous to them, not borrowed from a deity who happens to reside there.

The spring was there before the temple, and it is still running.

On sacred geography

The Core Distinction

Immanence vs. Transcendence

The most important structural distinction between non-theistic pagan nature religion and later theistic systems is the question of where sacred power is located. In theistic traditions, nature may be sacred — but derivatively so: a mountain is holy because God chose to reveal himself there, or because it is the dwelling of a deity. The sacrality is imported from elsewhere.

In pre-theistic nature religion, sacrality is immanent — indigenous to the place itself. The spring does not become sacred because a goddess of springs inhabits it. The spring is the power. It is not a symbol or a location chosen by a transcendent being. It is the thing itself, in its own right, deserving address and relationship.

This distinction — which philosopher of religion John Dewey described as the difference between the religious and religion — is the key to reading all the archaeological evidence for pre-theistic nature veneration without projecting later theistic categories backward onto it.

"There is a difference between a religion and the religious. A religion is a special body of beliefs and practices having some kind of institutional organization. The religious denotes attitudes that may be taken toward every object and every proposed end or ideal."

— John Dewey, A Common Faith (1934)

Sacred Waters

Springs, Rivers, and the Power of Water

Water is the most universally sacred natural phenomenon in ancient religion, and its veneration in pre-theistic form — before the naming of water deities — represents one of the most persistent and well-documented strata of non-theistic pagan practice.

Why Water Is Sacred

Springs emerge from the earth with no visible source — they appear to come from the underworld, from a realm beneath human perception. Rivers move, respond to season, flood and recede on their own logic. Rain transforms the landscape. The power and unpredictability of water, combined with its absolute necessity for life, made it a natural focus of sacred attention long before any water deity had a name.

Archaeological evidence for water veneration is among the oldest and most continuous in the record. The practice of depositing valuable objects in water — weapons, jewelry, food vessels, sometimes humans — is documented from the Neolithic through the Iron Age in Europe, and in the Near East from the Bronze Age onward.

Iron Age River Deposits: Britain and Continental Europe

The River Thames, the River Seine (the Sequana), the Rhine, and dozens of other European rivers have yielded enormous quantities of Iron Age votive deposits — objects that were deliberately thrown or placed in the water as offerings. The Thames alone has produced hundreds of fine bronze weapons, shields, helmets, and personal ornaments datable to the Bronze and Iron Ages.

The most famous single find is the Battersea Shield (c.c. 350–50 BCE), a ceremonially decorated bronze shield found in the Thames — far too fine for combat use, almost certainly made as an offering to the river's power. Similarly, the Waterloo Helmet, the River Witham Shield, and hundreds of swords show deliberate deposition, often with bent or "killed" blades — the object's utility destroyed before offering, concentrating its power in the gift.

Crucially, the earliest deposits — those from the Bronze Age — show no evidence of associated deity names or mythological narratives. The offering is to the river itself. Only in the later Iron Age and especially under Roman influence do we begin to see named presences (Sabrina for the Severn, Tamesis for the Thames, Sequana for the Seine) — early stages of personification.

Archaeological Reference

Miranda Green, Symbol and Image in Celtic Religious Art (1989) · Richard Bradley, The Passage of Arms: An Archaeological Analysis of Prehistoric Hoards and Votive Deposits (1990, 2nd ed. 1998) — the standard work on British votive deposit practice.

Sulis at Bath (Aquae Sulis)

The hot spring at what is now Bath, Somerset, is one of Britain's most remarkable sacred sites. The spring produces approximately 1.2 million liters of water per day at a constant 46°C — an extraordinary natural phenomenon that drew veneration far back into prehistory. Before the Romans built the spectacular temple complex (c.c. 60–70 CE), the spring was already a sacred site, its exact pre-Roman cult archaeology obscured by later building.

The Roman temple dedicated the spring to Sulis Minerva — a syncretism of a local Celtic power (Sulis) with the Roman Minerva. Sulis is not mentioned in any pre-Roman source; she appears at the moment of Roman encounter. This is the transition from non-theistic spring power to named deity caught almost in real time. The spring's sacred status preceded any name.

Sacred Groves

The Nemeton: The Holy Clearing

The Proto-Celtic word nemeton (cognate with Latin nemus, grove, and possibly with Greek nemos) refers to a sacred clearing in the forest — a place set apart by natural features or ritual designation where human and spiritual power could meet. It is among the most important categories of sacred space in Celtic and pre-Celtic European religion, and its significance predates any named deity.

Nemetons appear in placenames across the Celtic world: Nemetobriga (Spain), Drunemeton (Galatia, in modern Turkey — the sacred oak grove of the Galatian Celts), Medionemeton (Scotland), and the Gaulish site of Nemetacum (modern Arras, France). The distribution tracks the spread of Celtic cultures across Europe and Asia Minor from roughlyc. 800–300 BCE.

The Grove as Sanctuary

Classical sources describe Celtic sacred groves as places of awe, perhaps terror — spaces where the ordinary rules of human behavior did not apply and where powerful forces were present. The Roman poet Lucan (c. 60 CE), describing a grove near Massilia (Marseille) that Caesar's troops cleared, writes of a space where no bird nested, no animal sheltered, the boughs shuddered in still air, and darkness persisted even at midday.

This literary framing reflects real practice: nemetons were places where the veil between ordinary and sacred reality was thin. They required proper approach — ritual purification, the right season, the right person. The grove itself generated these conditions; no deity needed to be summoned or invoked.

Sacred Trees: Individual Power

Specific individual trees — not just groves — were also understood as concentrations of sacred power. The Irish bile (sacred tree, often an oak or ash) stood at the inauguration sites of kings and served as an axis between the human, underworld, and sky realms. To cut down a rival's bile was one of the gravest acts of war, striking at the spiritual heart of a people.

The Germanic Irminsul — a great pillar or tree sacred to the Saxons, destroyed by Charlemagne in 772 CE as a deliberate act of Christian conquest — was a similar axis-tree: the world-pillar connecting earth, underworld, and sky. The later mythological elaboration of Yggdrasil (the Norse world-tree) is a theistic overlay on this much older tree-pillar cult.

Sacred Heights

Mountain, Peak, and High Place

High places are universally associated with sky power across ancient religion — but in their pre-theistic form, the high place is the power rather than a place where a sky deity resides. Lightning strikes peaks; cloud gathers at summits; the boundary between earth and sky is literally thin. To stand on a mountain's top is to stand at the world's edge.

The Hebrew Bamot

The Hebrew word bamah (plural bamot) — usually translated "high place" — refers to outdoor sacred sites, often on hills or elevated ground, where the pre-exilic Israelites performed their sacrifices. The Hebrew Bible repeatedly condemns the bamot as sites of improper worship, a polemic that actually preserves evidence of their use: they were popular, widespread, and very difficult to suppress.

Importantly, the early bamot seem not to have been dedicated to any specific named deity — they were generalized sacred heights where sky and earth power could be accessed. The later association of specific bamot with Baal, Asherah, or Yahweh represents the theistic overlay on an older non-theistic high-place religion. This is among the clearest historical examples of the transition from nature-power to named deity.

Biblical Reference

Minoan Peak Sanctuaries (Crete, c. 2,000–1,450 BCE)

The Minoans of Bronze Age Crete maintained a distinctive category of sacred site: peak sanctuaries — open-air shrines at the summits of hills visible from settlements in the valleys below. Over twenty peak sanctuaries have been identified on Crete. They contain large quantities of terracotta votive figurines (human and animal), evidence of fire, and sometimes stone platforms or buildings.

What is notable is the apparent absence, in the earliest phase, of specific deity iconography. The votives appear to be offerings to the peak itself — the elevated vantage point with its connection to sky and its visibility from the community below. Later Minoan peak sanctuaries show increasing deity association (particularly a female figure of uncertain identity), but the earliest stratum is consistent with pure sacred-geography practice.

Standing Stones

Baetyli and Menhirs: The Inhabited Stone

Standing stones — single upright stones placed deliberately in the landscape — appear across cultures from the Neolithic Atlantic coast to Bronze Age Arabia, from prehistoric Korea to Iron Age Ethiopia. Their variety of function is wide, but a consistent thread runs through many traditions: the stone is not a marker or a monument. It is, or has become, an inhabited presence.

The Baetylus

The Greek word baetylus (from Phoenician/Semitic beth-el, "house of god") refers to sacred stones in the ancient Near East understood as the abode or concentration of divine power. The Phoenician and Canaanite traditions held certain unworked stones — especially meteorites — as intrinsically sacred, the power having arrived from outside the human world entirely.

The famous Black Stone of the Kaaba in Mecca is almost certainly a baetylus in origin — a pre-Islamic sacred stone that was incorporated into the Islamic tradition rather than eliminated. Pre-Islamic Arabian religion centered heavily on stone veneration, with sacred stones at shrines throughout the peninsula.

Atlantic Menhirs

The standing stones of Atlantic Europe — from the single menhir of Kerloas in Brittany (the tallest standing stone in France at 9.5 meters) to the avenues of Carnac (c. 4,700 BCE, over 3,000 standing stones in multiple alignments) — have resisted definitive interpretation for two centuries. Their functions include astronomical alignment, territorial marking, memorial, and gathering-place designation.

What is consistent across menhir traditions is the choice of specific stones: not any rock will do. Certain stones are selected, transported sometimes considerable distances, and erected with enormous communal labor. This selection and effort implies that specific stones were understood as having qualities — power, presence, significance — that others lacked. The menhir concentrates what was already in the stone; it does not create it.

The Sacred Hearth

Fire as Sacred Presence

Of all the natural phenomena venerated in ancient religion, fire is perhaps the most universal and the most ancient. The hearth fire was tended in Vedic India as Agni — a power that later acquired full divine status as the fire-god — but before Agni was a named deity, fire was simply present in the hearth as a sacred being requiring daily attention, feeding, and respect.

In many pre-literate cultures worldwide, the hearth fire is never allowed to go out, or it may be extinguished only in specific ritual circumstances and re-kindled through precise ceremony. The fire is a member of the household — older than any current occupant, inherited from ancestors, passed on to descendants. It is not a symbol of the divine; it is the divine presence in the home.

The Roman Vestal Virgins — who tended the eternal flame of Rome's civic hearth — are the institutionalized, state-level version of a household practice that long predated them. The civic fire of Rome was, at some deep structural level, the hearth fire of Rome's ancestral household, magnified to the scale of empire.

Chapter V

Shamanism

The shaman does not pray to a god. The shaman travels. Through altered consciousness, the specialist crosses into the spirit world, negotiates with its inhabitants, and returns bearing power, knowledge, or healing for the community.

The shaman does not pray. The shaman travels.

On ecstatic technique
c. 30,000
Years BCE — minimum age of the archaeological evidence for shamanic practice in Europe

Definition

What Shamanism Is

The word shaman comes from the Tungus-Evenki šaman, denoting a specific religious specialist among the indigenous peoples of Siberia. Russian explorers and Orthodox missionaries first recorded the term in the seventeenth century. It entered academic literature through M.A. Czaplicka's Aboriginal Siberia (1914) and was globalized by Mircea Eliade's 1951 synthesis.

A crucial and frequently missed point: shamanism is not a religion. It is a technique — a repeatable method for entering and navigating altered states on behalf of others. It has no creed, no scripture, and no deity requirement. It coexists comfortably with animism, ancestor cult, polytheism, and even with later monotheism in folk-practice form.

Core Practice

Voluntary Trance

The shaman deliberately enters an altered state — through drumming, fasting, isolation, or entheogens — and retains enough coherence to navigate purposively and report back.

Spirit Helpers

Power Animals & Allies

The shaman does not travel alone. Animal spirits and ancestral helpers accompany and assist. These are not gods but specific, personally known allies acquired through initiation ordeal.

Function

Community Service

Shamanic travel is not mystical tourism. It serves concrete ends: healing the sick, locating game, forecasting weather or enemies, escorting the dead, settling disputes with offended spirits.

Cosmology

Three-Level World

Classic shamanism operates in a layered cosmos: an upper world of sky powers, a middle world with its spirit shadow, and a lower world of the dead and the masters of animal species.

Shamanism is best understood as a technique of ecstasy — a method for entering modified states of consciousness in order to act within the spirit world on the community's behalf. It is older than any religion it accompanies.

— After Mircea Eliade, Shamanism: Archaic Techniques of Ecstasy (1951)

Eliade's Synthesis

The 1951 Synthesis and Its Critics

Eliade's Le Chamanisme et les techniques archaïques de l'extase (1951; English 1964) is among the most influential works of twentieth-century religious studies. He drew on Siberian ethnography, Central Asian archaeology, and comparative material from the Americas, Southeast Asia, Korea, and archaic Europe to argue that shamanism was a pan-human religious technology rather than a regional curiosity.

His central claim: the defining feature — controlled trance-journey through a structured spirit world — recurs with striking structural consistency across cultures with no contact whatsoever. That argued for deep cognitive universality rather than diffusion.

The Criticisms

Ronald Hutton (Shamans: Siberian Spirituality and the Western Imagination, 2001) argued that Eliade overclaimed. Many complexes labeled shamanic outside Siberia differ structurally in important ways, and Eliade's selection of evidence was shaped by prior commitment to a universal homo religiosus.

Alice Kehoe (Shamans and Religion, 2000) pressed harder: the category distorts indigenous traditions by stripping them from their contexts and flattening genuinely different practices into one template — with real political consequences for the communities so described.

These criticisms are well-founded and have reshaped the field. They do not, however, dissolve the core observation. Controlled trance-journeying for community benefit, with spirit helpers, within a layered cosmos, recurs across an extraordinary range of unrelated societies. The category needs discipline, not abandonment.

The Archaeology

Evidence for Prehistoric Shamanism

Lewis-Williams and the Neuropsychological Model

South African archaeologist David Lewis-Williams developed the most influential archaeological theory of Paleolithic shamanism, in work on San rock art and later in The Mind in the Cave (2002) and Inside the Neolithic Mind (with David Pearce, 2005). He drew on research into entoptic phenomena — the geometric forms generated by the visual cortex in altered states — to argue that the recurring abstract motifs in cave art (dots, grids, nested curves, spirals) are records of trance vision.

His model treats the cave itself as instrumental. Deep chambers are dark, silent, and disorienting: conditions that reliably induce hypnagogic imagery without any other intervention. On this reading the rock surface was understood as a membrane between worlds, and painting was less decoration than negotiation — marking the point where an animal power came through.

The cave wall was the membrane between the human and spirit worlds. Painting it was not decoration but passage — the mark left where something came through.

— After David Lewis-Williams, The Mind in the Cave (2002)

Key Sites

c. 40,000 BCE

Hohlenstein-Stadel, Germany

The Lion-Man: a human body with a lion's head, carved from mammoth ivory. The oldest known therianthrope, and the clearest possible evidence for a cognitive model in which human and animal identity interpenetrate.

c. 36,000 BCE

Chauvet Cave, France

Alongside its lions and rhinos, Chauvet carries a composite figure joining human and bison forms. The cave also contains chambers with pronounced acoustic resonance — consistent with drumming and chant at specific ritual stations.

c. 25,000 BCE

Dolní Věstonice, Czech Republic

A triple burial with unusual positioning and heavy ochre treatment, including an individual with a marked skeletal pathology. The care and singularity of the arrangement suggest interment of someone holding a specialist social role.

c. 12,000 BCE

Hilazon Tachtit Cave, Israel

An elderly woman buried with some fifty tortoise shells, a leopard's pelvis, an eagle's wing bone, a boar's foreleg, and a human foot. Published in PNAS (2008) as the oldest identified shaman burial — the assemblage matching the animal-part regalia of ritual specialists.

c. 9,500 BCE

Göbekli Tepe, Turkey

Monumental pillars carved with foxes, vultures, scorpions, and snakes — no named deities, no mythology, no writing. Consistent with a shamanic or totemic sanctuary mobilizing large groups long before settled agriculture.

Entheogenic Evidence

Residue analysis has begun to reveal the pharmacological dimension of ancient practice. Ephedra, cannabis, and opium poppy appear in Bronze Age ritual contexts across Eurasia. Residues from ritual vessels at Mas Castellar de Pontós in Catalonia (third century BCE) indicate beer infused with Hyoscyamus niger — henbane, a potent deliriant with a long ritual history in Europe.

The systematic ritual use of consciousness-altering plants is well attested and entirely consistent with the shamanic model. It is worth noting what the evidence does not show: entheogens are neither necessary nor sufficient for shamanic practice. Many traditions use none at all, relying on drum, cold, dark, and exhaustion.

Techniques

How the Shaman Travels

Drumming

The drum is the most widespread shamanic instrument — in Siberian traditions, the "horse" the shaman rides. Ethnographic and neurological work indicates that sustained rhythmic percussion in roughly the 4–7 Hz theta range reliably induces trance states in practiced subjects. The drum does not symbolize the journey. It produces the neurological condition in which the journey becomes possible.

Sensory Deprivation and Isolation

Prolonged isolation in dark, silent space — cave, sweat lodge, burial mound — generates vivid hypnagogic imagery as the brain amplifies internal signal in the absence of external input. This likely explains the preference for deep caves in the Paleolithic: they supply the necessary conditions with no pharmacology at all.

Entheogens as Teachers

Across shamanic traditions, specific plants are framed not as intoxicants but as teachers — persons in their own right who grant knowledge in exchange for correct relationship. Amanita muscaria in Siberia, peyote among the Huiçhol, ayahuasca in Amazonia, Salvia divinorum among the Mazatec: the framing is remarkably consistent and fits the animist premise exactly. Richard Evans Schultes documented over a hundred such plants in the Americas alone.

Ordeal

Extreme physical experience — fasting, cold exposure, sleeplessness, controlled pain — produces endorphin surges and dissociative states. Shamanic initiation across cultures typically involves such ordeals, frequently narrated as death and dismemberment followed by reassembly. The candidate must approach death to gain standing with the dead.

Chapter VIII

Sound, Music & Ecstatic Technique

Ritual is not silent. Sound is the one medium capable of filling a space, synchronizing a crowd, and altering consciousness directly — and the archaeological record shows that prehistoric peoples selected and engineered their sacred spaces for exactly that.

Strike the wall and something answers from behind it.

On cave acoustics
42,000
Years BCE — approximate age of the bone flutes from Geissenklösterle, the oldest known musical instruments

The Missing Dimension

Archaeology's Silent Bias

Archaeology is a visual discipline. It recovers shapes, surfaces, and spatial relationships, and it presents them in plans, sections, and photographs. Sound leaves no direct trace, and for most of the field's history it was simply not considered.

This produced a systematic distortion. Painted caves were studied as galleries — images to be looked at. Stone circles were studied as observatories — alignments to be sighted along. Both readings assume the eye is the relevant organ, and both may be substantially wrong.

Archaeoacoustics, developed since the late 1980s, asks a different question: what did these places sound like, and were they chosen or built for how they sound? The answers have been striking enough to reshape interpretation of several major site classes.

The Caves

Painting Where the Echo Is

In 1988 Iegor Reznikoff and Michel Dauvois published a study of three painted caves in the Ariège — Niaux, Portel, and Oxocelhaya. They moved systematically through each cave producing sustained vocal tones, mapping the points of maximum resonance, and then compared that acoustic map against the distribution of paintings.

The correlation was strong. Images clustered at resonant points. Some of the most acoustically active locations, too narrow or awkward to paint, carried red dots instead — marks requiring only a fingertip. The suggestion is that the marks record acoustic significance where images were impractical.

Later work extended the finding. Steven Waller documented that resonant chambers in painted caves frequently carry hoofed-animal imagery, and proposed that percussive echoes resemble the sound of a herd — that the cave, struck or shouted at, answers with thunder. Rupert Till and others have applied more rigorous instrumentation, confirming that the resonance is real while urging caution about the interpretation.

The cave answers. Strike the wall and a herd runs somewhere behind it. To a people who understood the rock as a membrane, the echo was not acoustics — it was reply.

— On the acoustic reading of Paleolithic cave art

This bears directly on the shamanic interpretation set out in Chapter VII. Deep caves supply, simultaneously, total darkness, sensory deprivation, thermal stability, and extraordinary acoustics. They are close to ideal instruments for inducing and structuring altered states. If the paintings mark where the spirit world was contacted, the acoustic data indicates that contact was substantially an auditory event.

Instruments

What They Played

c. 42,000 BCE

Bone Flutes

From Geissenklösterle and Hohle Fels in the Swabian Jura: flutes of griffon vulture radius and mammoth ivory, with finger holes and, in the ivory examples, carefully cut and sealed joins. The ivory instruments required splitting, hollowing, and rejoining a tusk — days of skilled work.

Widespread

Bullroarers

A slat of bone or wood whirled on a cord, producing a pulsing roar audible over kilometres. Examples are known from the Upper Paleolithic onward. Ethnographically among the most consistently sacred and secret instruments recorded, frequently restricted to initiates.

Inferred

Drums & Frame Drums

Almost never preserved, since hide and wood decay. Inferred from iconography, from the near-universal ethnographic association of drum with trance, and from the neurological effect of sustained rhythm in the theta range.

Lithophones

Ringing Stone

Stalactites and specific stone formations that resonate when struck. Percussion marks and ochre traces at Nerja in Spain and elsewhere indicate that particular formations were repeatedly struck — the cave itself played as an instrument.

The Swabian flutes deserve emphasis. They are contemporary with the Lion-Man and the earliest figurative art, and they are not crude. The Hohle Fels vulture-bone flute produces a usable scale. Music, image-making, and personal ornament all appear together in the same region and period — the archaeological signature of fully modern symbolic behaviour, arriving as a package.

Monuments

Building for Resonance

Stone Circles

Rupert Till and Bruno Fazenda's acoustic modelling of Stonehenge — using both the full-scale concrete replica at Maryhill in Washington State and computer reconstruction of the complete monument — found substantial reverberation within the stone setting. A complete circle of sarsens with lintels would have produced a markedly enclosed sound field, distinct from the surrounding open landscape.

The effect matters because it is a boundary that can be heard. Stepping inside the circle changes how voices and drums behave. For participants, the transition from profane to sacred space would have been audible as well as visible — a threshold registered by the body.

Passage Tombs and Chambers

Aaron Watson and David Keating's work at Camster Round and Maeshowe found that these chambers exhibit strong standing-wave behaviour at frequencies between roughly 95 and 120 Hz — squarely within the male vocal range. A group chanting inside such a chamber produces resonance that is felt physically as much as heard, and that can generate infrasound components associated with unease and altered perception.

The Hypogeum of Ħal Saflieni on Malta is the most extreme documented case. Its so-called Oracle Chamber, cut into the rock, amplifies low male vocal tones dramatically and distributes the sound through the complex. The chamber was carved, not found — meaning its acoustic properties were produced deliberately by people cutting stone with stone.

Resonance

95–120 Hz standing waves

Documented in multiple Neolithic chambers. Corresponds to the adult male voice. Produces physical sensation, spatial disorientation, and difficulty locating the sound's source.

Rhythm

4–7 Hz percussion

The theta range. Sustained drumming at this tempo reliably induces trance states in practised subjects — the neurological basis of the shamanic drum.

Infrasound

Below 20 Hz

Beneath conscious hearing but detectable by the body. Associated in laboratory studies with anxiety, chills, and reported sensations of presence. Generated incidentally by large resonant chambers and by bullroarers.

Why Sound Works

The Mechanisms

Entrainment

Rhythmic auditory stimulus draws neural oscillation toward its frequency. This is a measurable physiological effect, not a metaphor. Sustained drumming in the theta band shifts brain activity toward states associated with hypnagogia and imagery. The drum is a device for operating on the nervous system, and shamanic traditions that call it a vehicle are describing its function accurately.

Synchrony and Collective Feeling

Moving and vocalizing in time with others produces measurable increases in group cohesion, pain tolerance, and cooperative behaviour. Durkheim's collective effervescence — the sense of a force greater than oneself arising in assembled ritual — has a plausible mechanism here. Synchronized sound is among the most efficient means of producing it.

Source Ambiguity

In a strongly resonant space, sound cannot be localized. It appears to come from everywhere, or from the stone itself. For a worldview in which rock, cave, and earth are potential persons, an environment that returns voice from no identifiable direction is not an acoustic curiosity. It is a conversation.

Taken together these mechanisms explain why sound recurs at the centre of ritual practice across unconnected cultures. It synchronizes groups, alters consciousness directly, and makes the environment appear to respond. No other medium does all three.

Before the GodsIX. Sacrifice: Gift, Exchange & Violence

Chapter IX

Sacrifice: Gift, Exchange & Violence

To sacrifice is to destroy something valuable on purpose. Every theory of religion must explain why humans have done this, everywhere, for as long as there have been humans — and the explanations disagree profoundly.

To sacrifice is to destroy something valuable on purpose, and to mean it.

On the logic of offering

The Problem

Why Destroy What You Need?

Sacrifice is economically irrational on its face. A community with barely enough grain burns some of it. A herder with a small flock kills a healthy animal and does not eat all of it. A warrior takes a superbly made sword, bends it double so it can never be used, and drops it into a lake.

The behavior is close to universal, appears in the archaeological record from the Paleolithic onward, and persists into fully literate state religions. Any account of religion that cannot explain it is incomplete.

What follows sets out the main theories, then the archaeological evidence. It is worth saying in advance that no theory commands consensus, and that the practice is probably overdetermined — several mechanisms operating together rather than one cause.

Theories

Four Explanations

Gift and Exchange

The oldest and simplest account: sacrifice is a gift, and gifts create obligation. Hubert and Mauss's Sacrifice: Its Nature and Function (1898) established the framework, and Mauss's later The Gift (1925) generalized it. The offering establishes or maintains a relationship with a power; the power is thereby obligated to reciprocate.

The Latin do ut des — I give so that you may give — states it exactly. This model fits the non-theistic material especially well, because it requires no deity with intentions, only a relationship in which giving and receiving are structurally linked. One may give to a spring without believing the spring has plans.

Hubert and Mauss added a crucial structural observation: sacrifice mediates between the sacred and profane. The victim is consecrated, destroyed, and thereby transferred across a boundary the participants cannot cross themselves. The destruction is the transfer mechanism — which is why the offering must be genuinely lost, not merely dedicated.

Communion

William Robertson Smith, in Lectures on the Religion of the Semites (1889), proposed that sacrifice is fundamentally a shared meal. The community and the sacred power eat together; the offering is the medium of participation. Smith emphasized the totemic case, in which the animal consumed is the clan's own totem — making the meal an act of collective self-consumption and renewal.

Smith's specific totemic theory has not survived, but the communion element is real and visible archaeologically. Greek Olympian sacrifice divided the animal: the gods received bones and fat burned into smoke, the participants received the meat. The rite was simultaneously an offering and a feast, and for most participants the feast was the point.

Violence and Scapegoat

René Girard's Violence and the Sacred (1972) offered the most provocative modern theory. Human communities generate internal violence through mimetic rivalry — desiring what others desire. Left unchecked this violence escalates. Sacrifice discharges it onto a substitute victim who cannot retaliate, restoring peace. Religion, on Girard's account, originates in this mechanism.

The theory has been enormously influential in literary and theological circles and is regarded with considerable scepticism by anthropologists and archaeologists. Its principal weakness is that it explains too much: nearly any ritual can be redescribed as scapegoating. It also predicts a level of latent social crisis that small-scale societies do not obviously exhibit.

Hunting Guilt

Walter Burkert's Homo Necans (1972) — published the same year as Girard's book, and independently — located sacrifice in the psychology of the hunt. Paleolithic hunters killed animals they recognized as kin to themselves, and developed ritual to manage the resulting unease: apology to the prey, ceremonial disposal of bones, restitution to the animal's spirit or master.

When herding replaced hunting, the ritual outlived its original context and became sacrifice proper. Burkert pointed to the Greek Bouphonia — the ox-murder — in which the sacrificial axe was formally tried and found guilty of the killing, as a fossil of exactly this anxiety.

Burkert's account fits the ethnographic hunting evidence well: bear ceremonialism across the circumpolar north, whale ceremonies among Arctic peoples, and the widely documented practice of returning bones to water or earth so the animal can be reborn. Its weakness is the assumption that hunters universally feel guilt, which is not well supported.

The Evidence

Sacrifice in the Ground

Killed Objects

The clearest archaeological signature of sacrifice is deliberate destruction of value. Across Bronze and Iron Age Europe, weapons deposited in water were routinely bent, broken, or folded first — rendered permanently useless before being given up.

This is significant for interpretation. A sword thrown intact into a river might be lost, hidden, or discarded. A sword bent double and then thrown in was destroyed on purpose, and the destruction cost real wealth. Structured deposition of this kind is among the most secure ritual identifications available to archaeology.

The scale can be startling. At Hjortspring in Denmark, an entire war-boat with weapons for perhaps eighty men was deposited in a bog around 350 BCE. At Illerup Ådal, thousands of items of military equipment were destroyed and deposited across several episodes. These are not individual offerings but the systematic destruction of captured armies' equipment.

Animal Offerings

c. 70,000 BCE

Cave Bear Deposits, Alpine caves

Accumulations of cave bear skulls in Alpine caves were once read as evidence of a Neanderthal bear cult. Modern reassessment attributes most to natural accumulation and hibernation mortality. A useful reminder that the earliest claimed sacrifices are often the least secure.

c. 9,000 BCE

Göbekli Tepe, Turkey

Enormous quantities of butchered gazelle and aurochs bone, far exceeding what the non-residential site's builders would have needed. Consistent with large-scale feasting — sacrifice and communal meal as the mechanism that assembled the labour force.

c. 2,500 BCE

Durrington Walls, England

Massive pig-bone assemblages near Stonehenge, with isotope analysis showing animals driven from across Britain. Slaughter clustered at midwinter. Feasting on a scale that implies periodic gathering of dispersed communities.

c. 400 BCE

Danish bog deposits

Whole animals, pottery, wooden vessels, and food placed in bogs across Jutland — alongside human bodies. The same locations receive both, over centuries, suggesting a continuous category of offering rather than distinct practices.

Human Sacrifice

The evidence for prehistoric human sacrifice is real but easily overstated, and it demands the strictest application of the methodological caution set out in Chapter II. A body showing violence may be a sacrifice, an execution, a murder, or a war casualty. Distinguishing them requires context.

The northern European bog bodies provide the strongest cases. Tollund Man was hanged and then carefully placed. Lindow Man suffered a sequence of injuries — blows to the head, a garrote, a cut throat — sometimes called the triple death and paralleled in later Irish literary sources. The combination of overkill, formal deposition in a liminal place, and last meals of unusual composition points away from ordinary homicide.

Tacitus, writing in Germania (98 CE), describes Germanic peoples drowning certain offenders and offering victims in sacred groves. Roman sources on the Celts, including Caesar and the poet Lucan, describe human sacrifice in lurid detail — and must be handled with care, since portraying an enemy as barbarous served an obvious political purpose. Where archaeology and hostile literary testimony agree, the case is stronger; where only the literature asserts it, scepticism is warranted.

The bog gives back the body but not the reason. Everything we say about why Tollund Man died is inference from the manner of his death and the place of his deposition.

— On interpreting the bog bodies

Structure

The Anatomy of an Offering

Across cultures, sacrificial rites tend to share a common sequence. The pattern is stable enough to be diagnostic when identifying ritual deposits.

Phase 1

Selection & Setting Apart

The victim is chosen and marked as no longer ordinary — decorated, washed, garlanded, fed specially. It ceases to be property and becomes something dedicated.

Phase 2

Consent & Displacement

Responsibility is deflected. The animal is made to appear willing by nodding; the killing is blamed on the implement or a designated outsider. The community distances itself from the act it is performing.

Phase 3

Destruction

The irreversible moment — killing, burning, breaking, submersion. Value is genuinely lost. This is what separates sacrifice from display.

Phase 4

Division & Consumption

What remains is apportioned: a share to the power, a share to the participants, a share destroyed entirely. The distribution encodes the community's own hierarchy.

The second phase is the most revealing. The elaborate machinery of consent — the nodding ox, the guilty axe, the foreign executioner — indicates that participants experienced the killing as requiring justification. Something had to be managed. Burkert read this as inherited hunting anxiety; Girard read it as disguised communal violence. The evidence supports the observation without settling the explanation.

Non-Theistic Sacrifice

Offerings Without Recipients

A question central to this book: to whom is a sacrifice made, when there is no god?

The answer visible in the archaeological record is that the recipient is frequently a place rather than a person. Objects go into this bog, this river, this shaft. The deposition points are used repeatedly across centuries, often with remarkable spatial precision, while no image, name, or shrine identifies an addressee.

This is coherent within an animist framework. The bog is a power; it receives; the giver's obligation is discharged and the relationship maintained. No intention needs to be attributed to the bog, any more than one attributes intentions to a creditor when repaying a debt on time.

The transition to theistic sacrifice is visible in the same record. Later deposits begin to carry dedications, then names, then images. At Bath, the spring received offerings long before anything called Sulis appears in the sources; the inscribed curse tablets addressed to Sulis Minerva belong to the Roman phase. The practice is continuous; the addressee is added.

Chapter VI

Fetishism & Totemism

Power objects and clan animals — two fundamental modes of non-theistic sacred relationship that locate spiritual authority in specific objects and specific natural kinds, organizing both individual ritual life and collective social identity.

Animals are good to think with, not merely good to eat.

After Claude Lévi-Strauss

Fetishism

The Power Object: What a Fetish Actually Is

The word fetish has been distorted by centuries of colonial contempt and twentieth-century psychoanalytic appropriation. In its original religious studies meaning, a fetish is an object understood to contain, concentrate, or be a form of sacred power — not to represent or symbolize it, but to actually embody it. The distinction is critical: an icon of a saint represents the saint; a fetish is the power.

The Term's Origin

The word derives from Portuguese feitiço, "crafted thing" or "charm," which Portuguese traders used beginning in the 15th century to describe West African sacred objects they encountered. These were objects — carved figures, prepared bundles, special stones — that the local peoples treated as active, powerful participants in life: fed, consulted, punished when they failed to deliver, and propitiated when offended.

The Dutch scholar Willem Bosman (1703) and later Charles de Brosses (Du Culte des dieux fétiches, 1760) attempted to theorize fetishism as a religious category. De Brosses proposed it as the most primitive form of religion — predating even animism. His evolutionary framing is rejected, but his core observation — that treating material objects as intrinsically powerful (not merely symbolically significant) is a distinct and ancient religious orientation — stands.

"The fetish is not worshipped because it is a god. It is not a symbol of anything beyond itself. It is power — concentrated, localized, and addressable. The person who holds it holds something real."

— Paraphrase of Alfred Gell, Art and Agency (1998)

Alfred Gell's Anthropological Account

Anthropologist Alfred Gell's posthumous Art and Agency: An Anthropological Theory (1998) provides the most sophisticated modern account of why objects are treated as agents. Gell argued that objects can be understood as "secondary agents" — distributed extensions of the agency of their makers, owners, or the forces they embody. The fetish is not a mistake about the nature of matter. It is an accurate perception that objects carry causal power in social networks — they do things, they affect people, they change outcomes.

This is not so different from modern people's felt relationship to heirlooms, flags, or sacred texts. The difference is degree of explicitness and the scope of power attributed, not the basic cognitive structure.

Historical Examples

Fetish Objects Across Cultures

Roman

The Fascinum

A phallic amulet worn by Roman children and soldiers, believed to actively ward off the evil eye (fascinum). Not a symbol of a god's protection — the object itself was the apotropaic power. Thousands of examples survive in bronze and gold.

Near East

Baetyli — Sacred Meteorites

Unworked stones, especially meteorites, understood in Phoenician and Canaanite religion as intrinsically sacred — fallen from sky-realm, carrying cosmic power in their substance. The great Baetyl of Emesa (modern Homs, Syria) was a black conical stone that the emperor Elagabalus brought to Rome in 218 CE.

Germanic / Norse

Gand and Seiðr Staves

The Norse concept of gand — a magical object or sent-power — and the völva's iron-tipped staff suggest fetish-like objects through which power was concentrated and directed. The staff is not a symbol; it is the instrument through which the völva's spirit-power operates.

Celtic

Ritual Weapons

The "killed" weapons deposited in rivers and bogs — deliberately bent, broken, or folded before offering — were not merely gifts. The object's power was concentrated by its destruction; the weapon's usefulness-energy was released as a gift to the water's own power.

West African

Nkisi (Kongo)

The Kongo nkisi — a prepared container (carved figure, bundle, vessel) inhabited by a spirit power (simbi) — is the paradigmatic case study for fetish theory. The nkisi is activated, feeds on offerings, and dispenses power. Not a god; not a symbol. A living power-object.

Australian

Churinga / Tjurunga

Sacred stone or wooden objects of the Australian Aboriginal peoples, representing the dream-time ancestors. Kept in secret, handled only by initiated men, understood as the literally solidified essence of the Ancestor — a concentration of the dreaming power in material form.

Egyptian

Heart Scarabs

Carved scarabs placed over the heart of the dead, inscribed to keep the heart from testifying against its owner. The object does not represent protection — it performs it, acting on the wearer's behalf in a proceeding they cannot attend.

Anglo-Saxon

Cunning-Bags

Assembled bundles — herbs, bone, metal scrap, thread — carried against illness and ill intent. Value lay in correct assembly by the right hands, not in materials. The bag was made powerful, not found powerful.

Totemism

The Clan Animal: Identity, Kinship, and Natural World

Totemism is the identification of a human social group — a clan, lineage, or moiety — with a natural kind: most typically an animal species, but also plants, natural phenomena (rain, thunder, the sun), or minerals. The totem is understood not as a symbol of the group's identity but as a kin relation: the clan descends from the totem ancestor, or the clan and totem species share a nature, or the clan has a special custodial relationship with the totem species.

The Intellectual History

J.F. McLennan coined the term "totemism" in 1869. It was subsequently theorized by J.G. Frazer (Totemism and Exogamy, 4 vols., 1910), Émile Durkheim (The Elementary Forms of Religious Life, 1912), Sigmund Freud (Totem and Taboo, 1913), and most influentially by Claude Lévi-Strauss (Totemism, 1962; The Savage Mind, 1962).

Lévi-Strauss's account was decisive and largely ends the naive phase of totemism studies. He argued that totemism is not a religious phenomenon at all but a cognitive one: the use of natural species as a system of classification for human social groups. "Animals are good to think [with], not just good to eat." The totem animal provides a set of observable natural differences (bear vs. eagle vs. salmon) that map onto social differences (clan A vs. clan B vs. clan C), giving social structure a natural grounding.

"Totemic beliefs are not a form of proto-science or animistic confusion. They represent a perfectly coherent use of the natural world as a vocabulary for thinking about social relations."

— Claude Lévi-Strauss, Totemism (1962, trans. Rodney Needham)

Durkheim's Account: The Sacred as Society

Durkheim's analysis of Australian Aboriginal totemism in The Elementary Forms (1912) proposed that the totem is not merely a classification device but a sacred symbol — and that what is being symbolized is society itself. When the clan venerates the eagle totem, they are (unknowingly) venerating the clan's collective power. The sacred is the social, experienced as something greater than and external to the individual.

This explains the distinctive emotional power of totem ritual: the effervescent experience of collective ceremony in which individual identity dissolves into group identity is the origin of the sense of the sacred. The totem concentrates and externalizes this feeling, giving it a name and a form.

Totem Features

What Totemism Entails in Practice

Exogamy

The most universal feature of totemic systems is exogamy — the prohibition on marrying within your totem group. You cannot marry someone of your own clan because they are, in a real sense, your kin: you share the same ancestral nature. This makes the totem system simultaneously a kinship system and a social exchange system, regulating the flow of people between groups.

Totemic Taboo

Members of a totem clan typically cannot hunt, kill, or eat their totem animal — except in specific, highly ritualized circumstances (the totemic feast). This taboo is not merely symbolic. It encodes a practical ecological restraint: if every clan has a different animal under its protection, and is prohibited from casual killing of that animal, the result is distributed protection of multiple species by different segments of the community.

Anthropologist Marshall Sahlins and ecologist Paul Shepard both argued that totemic taboos functioned as indigenous wildlife management — not through conscious conservation planning, but through the religious structuring of an appropriate relationship with specific animal populations. The result was similar: the animals of a region were distributed among the protective custodianship of different human groups.

Totemic Art and Architecture

The visual tradition of totem representation is among the most ancient in the archaeological record. The animal carvings of Göbekli Tepe (c. 9,500 BCE), the ivory animal figurines of the European Upper Paleolithic, and the extraordinary animal imagery of the cave paintings all potentially represent totemic relationships — the clan's ancestral animal rendered in durable form.

The Northwest Coast totem poles of indigenous North American peoples are the most elaborately developed totemic art tradition, encoding genealogical information, alliance histories, and spirit relationships in vertical stacked animal-figure iconography. Though these are not ancient (most surviving poles date to the 18th–19th century), they likely represent a tradition of genealogical-animal iconography reaching back considerably further.

Chapter XI

Divination & Reading the World

If the world is populated by powers, it can be consulted. Divination is the technology of asking a question and receiving an answer — and in non-theistic religion it requires no god to answer, only a world that is legible.

The question is not whether it works. The question is what it accomplishes.

On divination

The Premise

A World That Can Be Read

Divination assumes that events are connected — that the pattern of cracks in a heated bone, the flight of birds, or the fall of marked sticks corresponds to something otherwise unknowable. It does not assume a message-sender. The correspondence may simply be how the world is structured.

This distinction matters for the argument of this book. Prayer requires a listener. Divination requires only a legible world. It is therefore fully coherent within a non-theistic framework, and it is among the most widely attested religious activities in human history — present in every documented culture, and inferable well back into prehistory.

The anthropologist Philip Peek framed divination as a knowledge system rather than a superstition: a socially sanctioned method for producing decisions under uncertainty, whose authority derives from being outside any individual's control. That framing explains its persistence better than accounts that treat it as failed prediction.

Typology

Ways of Asking

Divinatory methods fall into a small number of structural types, which recur independently across unconnected cultures.

Type 1

Sortilege — Casting Lots

Marked objects thrown and read by how they fall: sticks, stones, bones, knucklebones, cut twigs. Randomizing devices, deliberately removed from human control. The oldest and simplest form, and the hardest to detect archaeologically.

Type 2

Extispicy — Reading Entrails

Examination of a sacrificed animal's organs, especially the liver. Highly developed in Mesopotamia and Etruria, where the liver was mapped into zones corresponding to regions of the cosmos. Combines sacrifice and consultation in one act.

Type 3

Augury — Reading Behaviour

Interpretation of animal behaviour, especially birds: species, direction of flight, calls, timing. Central to Roman public religion, where no significant state action proceeded without the auspices being taken.

Type 4

Pyromancy & Scapulimancy

Heating a bone or shell until it cracks, then reading the fractures. Independently developed across Eurasia and North America. Chinese oracle bones from the Shang dynasty are the best-documented instance — and produced the earliest Chinese writing.

Type 5

Incubation — Dream Seeking

Sleeping in a designated place to receive a dream. Practised at Greek healing sanctuaries, in Celtic and Germanic contexts, and widely in indigenous traditions. The place, not a deity, is what makes the dream significant.

Type 6

Possession & Oracular Speech

A practitioner enters altered consciousness and speaks with another voice. The Delphic Pythia is the famous case; the Norse völva performing seiðr is a northern parallel. Overlaps directly with shamanic technique.

Type 7

Hydromancy & Reflection

Reading water surfaces, springs, or reflective objects. Associated with sacred springs across Europe, connecting divination to the water-veneration described in Chapter VI.

Type 8

Ordeal

A test whose outcome determines truth or guilt — grasping hot iron, submersion, ingesting a toxin. Divination applied to justice: the world, not the judge, decides.

Evidence

Divination in the Record

Oracle Bones — the Documented Case

Shang dynasty China (c. 1250–1046 BCE) provides the only prehistoric-adjacent divination system we can read directly. Ox scapulae and turtle plastrons were thinned, pitted, and touched with a heated brand until they cracked. The diviner read the cracks, and the question and outcome were then inscribed on the bone itself.

Over 150,000 inscribed fragments have been recovered from Anyang. They record questions about harvests, weather, illness, childbirth, warfare, and the disposition of ancestors. Crucially, they show divination as routine administrative practice, performed continuously and archived — not an exceptional recourse in crisis.

The oracle bones matter methodologically as well. Scapulimancy is attested archaeologically across Eurasia and North America through characteristic burn marks and cracking on scapulae. In every case but China, the questions are lost. The Shang material shows what the silent evidence elsewhere represents.

The Etruscan Liver

The bronze Liver of Piacenza, found in 1877 and dating to around 100 BCE, is a life-sized model of a sheep's liver divided into forty regions, each inscribed with a divine name. It is a functional instrument: the practitioner mapped the features of a real liver against the model to determine which power was implicated.

The Etruscan system, transmitted to Rome as the disciplina etrusca, treated the liver as a microcosm — its regions corresponding to divisions of the sky. Divination here is not message-reception but correspondence-reading: the same structure appears at every scale, so the small can be read for information about the large.

Northern Europe

Tacitus describes Germanic lot-casting in Germania 10: strips cut from a fruit-bearing branch, marked, scattered on white cloth, and drawn three times while looking upward. He also records the observation of horses kept in sacred groves, whose movements and neighing were read — and notes that the Germans regarded the horses as knowing more than the priests.

Tacitus is a hostile-adjacent source writing for a Roman audience and using Germanic virtue as a critique of Roman decadence, so his details require caution. But lot-casting is attested independently in Norse sources through the practice of hlautteinn — blood-twigs used in sacrifice and divination — and the runic alphabet itself carried divinatory associations in later tradition, though the evidence for systematic rune-divination in the pre-Christian period is thinner than popular accounts suggest.

The Germans keep white horses at public expense in the sacred woods, untouched by any mortal work; the priests attend them and observe their neighing and snorting. No form of divination is more trusted.

— Paraphrasing Tacitus, Germania 10 (98 CE)

Function

What Divination Actually Does

The question of whether divination works is less interesting than the question of what it accomplishes, which is considerable and mostly independent of predictive accuracy.

Randomization as a Strategy

Omar Moore's classic 1957 study of Naskapi caribou hunting proposed that scapulimancy served as a randomizing device. Burned bone cracks unpredictably; the cracks determined the hunting direction. This prevented hunters from over-exploiting recently successful areas and stopped the caribou from adapting to predictable human patterns.

Moore's specific case has been questioned, but the general principle holds and has been formalized in game theory: against an adaptive opponent, unpredictable strategy outperforms any fixed rule. Divination supplies unpredictability that no individual could produce deliberately, since deliberate randomness is psychologically almost impossible.

Distributing Responsibility

A decision reached by divination is not any person's decision. In small communities where leaders must live indefinitely with those affected by their choices, this is valuable. If the outcome is bad, no faction was responsible. The procedure absorbs blame that would otherwise fracture the group.

Producing Commitment

Divination converts deliberation into decision. Groups deadlocked between options can remain deadlocked indefinitely; a procedure that yields an answer and carries collective authority ends the deliberation and enables coordinated action. In circumstances where acting decisively matters more than acting optimally, this is a real advantage.

Surfacing What Is Known

Interpretation is rarely mechanical. The practitioner reads ambiguous material in a specific social context, usually with detailed knowledge of the parties and the situation. The result frequently articulates what the community already suspected but could not say directly. The divinatory frame permits difficult truths to be voiced without any individual owning them.

Chapter XII

Taboo, Purity & Prohibition

Much of pre-theistic religion consists not of what must be done but of what must not. Prohibition requires no deity to issue it — only a world in which certain contacts are dangerous, and a community that knows which.

Dirt is matter out of place.

Mary Douglas

The Word

Tapu and the Anthropology of Prohibition

Taboo entered English from Polynesian tapu or tabu, recorded by James Cook in Tonga in 1777. Cook noted that the word covered things forbidden, but also things sacred, set apart, and dangerous — a range that puzzled him and that turned out to be the concept's most important feature.

Tapu does not distinguish holy from unclean. Both are conditions of being charged — removed from ordinary handling, requiring specific procedures for approach. Its opposite is not "permitted" but noa: ordinary, free, safe to touch.

This structure recurs widely. The Latin sacer means both consecrated and accursed. The Hebrew qadosh means set apart, not morally good. Greek hagos covers both sanctity and pollution. In each case a single term marks the charged state, and the moral valence is a later development.

The sacred and the unclean are not opposites. They are the same category viewed from different distances — both name what cannot be handled casually.

— On the structure of the sacred-profane distinction

Mary Douglas

Dirt as Matter Out of Place

Mary Douglas's Purity and Danger (1966) is the single most influential analysis of pollution belief, and it reframed the subject decisively. Douglas argued that pollution rules are not primitive hygiene, nor arbitrary superstition, but a by-product of classification.

Her formulation: dirt is matter out of place. Shoes are not dirty; shoes on the dining table are. Soil is not dirty; soil on the bedsheets is. Dirt exists only relative to a system of order, and it is precisely what that system cannot accommodate.

Pollution beliefs therefore mark the boundaries and failures of a culture's categories. What is unclean is what falls between recognized kinds, straddles them, or violates them. This explains the otherwise baffling specificity of dietary and contact rules.

Douglas's celebrated worked example is the Levitical dietary law. Animals are clean if they fully exemplify their class: land animals should be cloven-hoofed ruminants, water animals should have fins and scales, and so on. The pig is unclean not because of trichinosis but because it is cloven-hoofed yet does not chew the cud — an incomplete member of its category. Shellfish are unclean because they live in water without swimming.

Her reading has been criticized — notably for imposing more systematic coherence than the texts support, and for underweighting historical contingency. But the central insight, that pollution tracks classificatory anomaly rather than physical danger, has held up well and applies far beyond the Hebrew case.

Categories

What Gets Prohibited

Across unconnected cultures, the same domains attract prohibition with striking regularity. The consistency suggests that certain situations are structurally problematic for any system of classification.

Domain 1

Death & the Corpse

Contact with the dead pollutes almost universally. Handlers are secluded, then cleansed. The corpse is a person that is also an object — the most fundamental category violation available.

Domain 2

Blood & Birth

Menstruation and childbirth attract seclusion rules across a vast range of societies. Both involve bodily boundaries breached, substances crossing from inside to outside, and one person becoming two.

Domain 3

Food & Eating

Prohibited species, prohibited combinations, prohibited preparation. Eating is the deliberate incorporation of the outside world into the body — the boundary crossing that happens several times daily.

Domain 4

Sacred Places & Objects

Groves not entered, stones not touched, chambers restricted to initiates, regalia handled only by designated persons. Access is controlled because contact is consequential.

Domain 5

Names & Speech

Names of the dead unspoken, certain words avoided at sea or on the hunt, sacred formulae restricted to initiates. Speech acts on the world, so some utterances are too dangerous to make.

Domain 6

Persons in Transition

Initiates, mourners, warriors returning from killing, women in childbed. Anyone mid-passage between social states is temporarily anomalous — and temporarily set apart.

Domain 7

Kinship & Sex

Incest prohibition is the most universal human rule. Totemic exogamy extends it: one may not marry within the clan because clan members share a nature.

Domain 8

Mixing Kinds

Prohibitions on hybrid cloth, mixed crops, cross-species yoking. Anything that combines categories which the system holds apart. The Levitical rules against mixtures are the clearest example.

Miasma

Pollution in the Greek World

Greek religion supplies the best-documented pre-theistic pollution system in Europe, because it survived into the literate period with its logic intact. Robert Parker's Miasma (1983) remains the standard treatment.

Miasma is contagious ritual pollution. It is contracted by contact with death, birth, bloodshed, and certain sacred objects. It is not sin: it can be incurred accidentally, by the innocent, and even by the just. What matters is contact, not intent.

Its consequences are practical and social. The polluted may not enter sanctuaries or participate in sacrifice. Pollution spreads by association, which is why a polluted person endangers the whole community and why cities could be held collectively liable for an unpurified killing.

The Athenian regulations at Delos illustrate the logic: no one was permitted to die or give birth on the sacred island. Those approaching death or labour were removed to a neighbouring island. This is not squeamishness — it is boundary maintenance for an entire landmass treated as consecrated space.

Purification (katharsis) restores the ordinary state. Water, blood, fire, sulphur, and the passage of time are the standard agents. Notably, the procedures are technical rather than moral. One does not repent of miasma; one washes.

The Tragic Case

Sophocles' Oedipus Tyrannus turns on exactly this structure. Thebes suffers plague because it harbours an unpurified pollution. Oedipus is guilty of parricide and incest committed in complete ignorance — and his ignorance is irrelevant to the pollution, though it matters greatly to how we judge him.

The play is intelligible only if pollution is understood as contact rather than culpability. A modern reader looking for a moral failing to justify the outcome will misread it. The Greeks were watching a man discover that he is charged, not that he is wicked.

Function

Why Prohibition Persists

Marking Structure

Taboo makes a society's classification system visible and enforceable. Every observance rehearses the boundaries: this is food and that is not, this person is available and that one is not, this ground may be walked on and that may not. The rules teach the categories by requiring them to be performed.

Signalling Commitment

Costly observance is a reliable indicator of group membership. Rules that are difficult, inconvenient, or economically burdensome cannot be faked cheaply. Dietary restriction in particular is continuous, public, and expensive — which is exactly what makes it an effective marker of belonging.

Managing Genuine Danger

Some prohibitions do track real hazard: corpse handling and disease transmission, certain food-spoilage patterns, the risks of childbirth. The functionalist reading that treats all taboo as encoded hygiene overreaches badly — it cannot explain mixed-fabric rules — but the overlap is not zero, and the two accounts are not exclusive.

Distributing Restraint

Totemic food prohibitions, discussed in Chapter X, spread protection across species: each clan refrains from its own totem, so no species faces uniform pressure from the whole community. Whether or not this is why the rules exist, it is a consequence they produce.

Non-Theistic Structure

Prohibition Without a Lawgiver

This is the chapter's central point for the book's argument. Taboo requires no commanding deity. There is no legislator, no covenant, and no promised judgment.

The sanction is not punishment but consequence. Touching what is charged produces harm the way touching fire produces harm — automatically, impersonally, and regardless of intention or worthiness. The polluted person is not guilty; they are contaminated, and they must be cleaned before rejoining ordinary life.

The contrast with commandment religion is sharp. A commandment is issued by someone, may be disobeyed, and creates guilt. A taboo describes how the world behaves, may be violated accidentally, and creates danger. The first requires a person on the other end; the second requires only a structured cosmos.

Historically, the two blend. The Hebrew purity code sits alongside covenantal law; Greek miasma coexists with Olympian religion. But the underlying logic of pollution is older than the theology layered onto it, and it remains recognizable wherever a community treats certain contacts as dangerous rather than disobedient.

Chapter VII

Chthonic Religion

The oldest sacred relationship of agricultural humanity is not with the sky but with the earth beneath it — the darkness from which life emerges, into which the dead descend, and from which all nourishment returns. This is religion oriented not upward but downward.

What goes down into the dark comes back changed, and feeds us.

On chthonic reciprocity

The Word

Khthonios: Of the Earth

The Greek adjective khthonios (χθόνιος) means "of the earth" or "under the earth" — from khthōn, earth as substance, soil, ground (distinct from gē, the earth as surface or geographic entity, and oura, earth as territory). The chthonic is the deep earth: the layer beneath the surface where the dead go, where seeds germinate in darkness, where water moves in unseen channels, where the power that makes things grow resides in its most concentrated form.

Chthonic religion is thus organized around the powers below — not heaven, not the surface, but the underworld. In its pre-theistic form, these powers are not yet Hades or Persephone — they are simply the chthonioi, "the subterranean ones," addressed directly through material offerings passed into the ground itself.

"The Olympians belong to later layers of Greek religion. Beneath them, reaching back into the Neolithic, is a wholly different religious world: dark, chthonic, concerned with the dead and with fertility, organized around the pit rather than the altar."

— Walter Burkert, Greek Religion (1977, trans. Raffan 1985)

The Pit Offering

The Bothros: Feeding What Is Below

The central ritual device of chthonic religion is the bothros (βόθρος) — a pit or trench dug in the earth into which offerings are poured or placed. Blood, wine, grain, oil, honey, milk — liquids especially — were appropriate offerings, as they could percolate down through the soil to reach the underworld powers. Solid offerings — animals, grain cakes, objects — were also deposited, but the pit-pouring of liquid is the defining gesture.

The bothros appears in Homer's Odyssey (Book XI), where Odysseus digs a pit on the instructions of Circe and pours into it "a libation to all the dead — first with honey and milk, then with sweet wine, then with water; over all of it I sprinkled white barley." The dead flock to the blood of the sacrificed sheep; by drinking it they temporarily recover the ability to speak and remember. This is a literary formalization of an older pre-literary practice.

Archaeological Evidence for Pit Deposits

Ritual pit deposits appear throughout the Neolithic Near East. At Çatalhöyük (c. 7,500 BCE), animal bone assemblages beneath house floors suggest regular offerings passed into the earth — feeding the chthonic powers beneath the domestic space. At Pre-Pottery Neolithic sites in the Levant, carefully arranged pit deposits of animal bones, beads, and other materials occur beneath house floors and in special-purpose pits.

In Bronze Age Greece, the megaron — the central hall of the Mycenaean palace — frequently had a sunken area or pit beneath it with evidence of repeated burning and deposition. These antedate the Olympian pantheon and represent a chthonic palace-cult that Walter Burkert traces to Minoan and pre-Minoan substrates.

The Greek Thesmophoria festival — the most ancient and widespread women's festival in the Greek world, celebrated in honor of Demeter — included a rite called the Descent: pigs, pine cones, and cakes were thrown into underground chambers (megara, a term distinct from the palace room), then retrieved after several days in a state of putrefaction. The rotted matter was mixed with seed grain to ensure a good harvest. This is pure chthonic logic: what descends into the earth-below returns transformed, carrying the power of the underground.

Pre-Olympian Greece

The Chthonic Stratum Beneath Greek Religion

Greek religion as it appears in Homer and Hesiod — the Olympian pantheon with its personal narratives, genealogies, and anthropomorphic character — is a relatively late development layered over a much older substratum. Scholars including Walter Burkert, Martin Nilsson, and Jane Ellen Harrison have traced this older layer through cult practice, festival calendar, and the structure of myth.

Jane Ellen Harrison and the Chthonioi

Classical scholar Jane Ellen Harrison (1850–1928), in Prolegomena to the Study of Greek Religion (1903) and Themis (1912), made the foundational argument for the priority of chthonic religion in Greek antiquity. Harrison distinguished between the Olympians (the bright sky-gods of Homer, received from without, associated with civic religion and the polis) and the chthonioi (the dark underworld powers indigenous to Greece, associated with earth, death, fertility, and women's cult).

Harrison argued that the chthonioi are older — they represent the Neolithic substrate of Greek religious life, the religion of people who buried their dead beneath their houses and performed rituals to the powers below long before Greek-speaking peoples arrived in the Aegean. The Olympians, she proposed, were in part an intrusion or overlay from Indo-European sky-religion, partially displacing but never fully eliminating the older chthonic system.

"The Olympians are in a sense immigrants. The chthonioi were there first. And they never left — they went underground, sometimes literally, and continued to receive their due in the darkness outside the civic festivals."

— Jane Ellen Harrison, Prolegomena to the Study of Greek Religion (1903)

Chthonic Sacrifice: The Enagismata

The ritual difference between Olympian and chthonic cult in ancient Greece was explicit and structural. Olympian sacrifice used a high altar (bomos); the animal was killed facing upward; the meat was shared between gods and humans. Chthonic sacrifice used a low pit or ground-level hearth (eschara); the animal was killed facing downward or toward the earth; the meat was entirely consumed — burned completely, or poured in as blood, or buried — because the chthonic powers do not share food with the living. The offerings are gifts that go entirely below.

These offerings — the enagismata (sg. enagisma, "offerings poured into a sacred space") — were made to heroes, ancestors, and the unnamed dead. They predate and exist in parallel with Olympian cult throughout the historical period.

The Erinyes / Eumenides

The Erinyes — the Furies — are among the most purely chthonic figures in Greek mythology. They are the enforcers of blood-kin obligation, living in the underworld, and they arise from the spilled blood of murdered kin. They have no Olympian parent, no mythology of birth — they simply exist as the power of chthonic vengeance. Aeschylus's Oresteia dramatizes the tension between the old chthonic justice (blood demands blood, the Erinyes are satisfied by nothing less) and the new civic/Olympian justice (rationalized law, overseen by Athena). The Erinyes' transformation into the Eumenides ("Kindly Ones") at the play's end represents the symbolic domestication of chthonic religion by the polis — but also its preservation beneath a new name.

Underworld Logic

The Structure of Chthonic Cosmology

The Underworld as the Source of Fertility

The most important insight of chthonic religion is the identification of the underworld — the realm of the dead — with the source of agricultural fertility. This is not a metaphor: it is a precise observation. Seeds go into the dark earth and emerge transformed as life. The dead go into the dark earth. The dead and the seeds share the same realm. Therefore, the dead can influence the harvest; therefore, feeding the dead is connected to feeding the living.

This logic is explicit in the most important Greek chthonic myth: the story of Persephone (in its oldest recoverable form, well before the Homeric Hymn to Demeter formalized it). Persephone descends into the earth, the earth becomes barren above, she returns, the earth revives. This is not allegory — it is the cosmological claim that life above requires the right relationship with what is below.

Serpent Symbolism

The serpent is the universal chthonic animal — it lives in the earth, emerges from it, returns to it. Its shedding of skin makes it a symbol of death and renewal simultaneously. Serpent symbolism appears at every site of chthonic religion: the snake-holding figures of Minoan Crete, the serpent beneath the Athenian Acropolis (sacred to Erichthonius, the chthonic ancestor of Athens), the great serpent-mound building tradition of prehistoric North America.

The serpent is not a deity — it is a manifestation of chthonic power, a visible form of what lives below. Offerings to the chthonic powers were frequently presented to the serpent that lived in the sacred precinct; the serpent received on behalf of the underground as its representative.

Greek

Erichthonius / Erectheus

The autochthonous ancestor of Athens — literally "born from the earth." His lower half was serpentine. A living serpent was kept on the Acropolis as his embodiment; when the Persians sacked Athens (480 BCE) and the snake disappeared, the Athenians read it as a sign that the city's chthonic ancestor had withdrawn his protection.

Minoan

Snake Goddess Figurines

The famous Minoan "snake goddess" figurines from Knossos (c. 1,600 BCE) show a female figure holding serpents in each hand. These are chthonic power objects, not representations of a named deity — the snakes embody the underworld power that the figure (priestess or goddess) commands.

Norse

Níðhöggr

The great serpent gnawing at the roots of Yggdrasil in Norse mythology is a late mythological formalization of the chthonic serpent — the power that lives at the earth's deepest root, consuming the world-tree's foundation. In the oldest form this is simply the serpentine chthonic power beneath the world-axis.

Anatolian

Serpents at Göbekli Tepe

Snakes are among the most frequently carved animals on the T-pillars — often descending the stone vertically, head downward. On the oldest monumental architecture known, the serpent already marks the downward direction.

Chapter VIII

Cosmology & Worldview

The universe of ancient non-theistic paganism is not created, not governed, and not moving toward an end. It is a structured, layered, cyclical world where power flows through relationship and the human task is maintenance, not salvation.

The world is not going anywhere. It is turning.

On cyclical cosmology

The Structure of the World

Three Worlds: Up, Middle, Below

The most cross-culturally consistent cosmological feature of pre-theistic religion is the three-level world: an upper realm, a middle realm where humans live, and a lower realm. It appears in Siberian shamanism, Norse cosmology, Vedic tradition, Mesopotamian religion, Mesoamerican thought, and the archaeology of Neolithic Europe. Too many of these are unconnected for diffusion to explain it. It appears instead to be a basic human mapping of the experienced environment: sky above, ground below, life between.

Critically, in pre-theistic form the upper and lower worlds carry no moral charge. They are not heaven and hell, not reward and punishment. They are domains of different power. Sky power (cyclical, distant, governing timing) and earth power (chthonic, generative, immediate) stand in no ethical opposition. Both are necessary; both require maintenance.

Above

The Upper World

Sun, moon, stars, weather. Its most important manifestation is the seasonal cycle. Not yet Zeus or Odin — simply the great alternation of light and dark, warmth and cold, which sets the ritual and agricultural calendar.

Between

The Middle World

The surface where humans, animals, and plants live — and where nature spirits and wandering dead occupy the same space in a different register. This is the realm whose spirit-shadow the shaman navigates.

Below

The Lower World

The earth's interior: realm of the dead, roots of things, source of fertility. Not a punishment realm. The dead go there because that is where the dead go, and from there the growing power rises each season.

Connecting

The Axis Between

The three realms are useless without passage between them. Tree, pillar, mountain, or cave shaft — the axis is what makes the cosmos navigable rather than merely layered, and it is the shaman's road.

The World Axis

The three worlds are joined by a world axis — tree, pillar, mountain, or cave shaft — along which movement between realms occurs. The shaman ascends or descends it in trance; the dead descend it; sky power comes down it as lightning and rain.

The axis appears as Yggdrasil in Norse myth, the World Tree or World Mountain in Siberian practice, Meru in Vedic cosmology, the ceiba as axis mundi in Mesoamerica. It is not a god. It is the structural spine of a cosmos organized horizontally by human territory and vertically by these layered domains.

The shaman's pole does not symbolize the world-tree. Within the practice it is the world-tree — a real path of ascent, navigable by anyone trained to climb it.

— After Mircea Eliade, Shamanism (1964)

Time

Cyclical Time and the Ritual Year

The temporal structure of this cosmology is cyclical. There is no creation, no midpoint revelation, no apocalypse. There is the wheel: day, lunar month, agricultural year, human generation, and in some traditions great astronomical cycles beyond those.

This is not fatalism. It is the recognition that the world sustains itself through repetition — and, crucially, that human ritual participation is part of what keeps the cycle turning. The solstice must be witnessed. The dead must be fed in season. First fruits must be offered before anything is eaten. Ritual is not commemorative ceremony; it is maintenance work on the cosmos.

The Eight-Point Year

Pre-theistic European ritual life organized around four solar stations — two solstices, two equinoxes — and four cross-quarter days between them. The eight-spoked wheel representing this cycle is among the oldest recurring symbols in European material culture, appearing on Bronze Age pottery and rock carving well before association with any named deity.

Feb Cross-Quarter

Imbolc — Return of Light

The first perceptible lengthening of days and the first lactation of ewes. The ritual task is acknowledging the growing season before it visibly arrives — an act of trust in the cycle.

Spring Equinox

Balance — Planting

Day and night equal; the growing season opens. Offerings to the earth below for a productive year, and the first turning of the soil.

May Cross-Quarter

Beltane — Full Life

Fire festivals across Celtic and Germanic Europe. Cattle driven between paired fires for purification before summer pasture, the community following after. Among the best-attested pre-Christian fire rites in Britain and Ireland.

Summer Solstice

Midsummer — Peak

The longest day. Bonfires; medicinal plants gathered at maximum potency. Stonehenge's principal axis addresses this sunrise — the monument organized around a cosmic moment, not a named god.

Aug Cross-Quarter

Lughnasadh — First Harvest

The first grain cut. First-fruits offerings made before any is consumed — give to below before taking from above. Chthonic reciprocity in its plainest form.

Autumn Equinox

Balance — Second Harvest

Harvest completed, stores laid in, the balance tilting toward dark. Thanksgiving and provisioning.

Nov Cross-Quarter

Samhain — The Thin Night

At the year's turn the boundary between living and dead grows thin. The dead may return and are met with food and fire. The direct ancestor of Hallowe'en. No deity is required: the calendar itself makes the opening.

Winter Solstice

Midwinter — Longest Night

Deepest dark and the promise of return. Newgrange is built to admit light to its chamber at this dawn. Yule fires burn across the Germanic north. The task is to witness and support the turn.

Ethics

Reciprocity as the Moral Core

The ethics of this cosmology rest on neither commandment nor revelation but on reciprocity: every relationship carries mutual obligation, and failure to honor it produces consequences — not as divine punishment, but as the ordinary result of a broken relationship.

Do ut Des

The Latin formula do ut des — I give so that you may give — captures the logic. The offering is not self-abasement before an omnipotent being. It is a gift within an ongoing exchange. You give to the spring so the spring keeps flowing. You give to the ancestor so the ancestor keeps watch. You offer first fruits so the field produces again.

This extends seamlessly into social life. Communities exchange gifts with each other and with the non-human world on the same terms. The ethics of the natural world and of human society share one structure — which is precisely why the distinction between "religion" and "everyday conduct" is hard to draw in these traditions.

The gift is never free. To give is to obligate; to receive is to be obligated. The principle governs exchange between the living and the dead, between people and place, as much as between persons.

— After Marcel Mauss, The Gift (1925)

Pollution and Purification

These traditions also carry a concept of miasma — pollution — but not in a moral sense. Miasma is not sin. It is contact-danger acquired through proximity to the sacred-dangerous: the dead, blood, birth, liminal states. Purification (katharsis) does not improve the person morally; it restores appropriate distance.

There is no original sin here, no permanent stain, no judge. There is boundary management: knowing when you have come too close to concentrated power, and performing what restores safe separation.

Wyrd — Structure Without a Designer

In Germanic tradition, wyrd (Old English) or urðr (Old Norse) names what has become — the accumulated weight of what has already happened, constraining what can happen next. Every act sends consequence through the web; past action shapes present possibility; the threads can be navigated skillfully but not escaped.

The Norns who tend this web are neither gods nor quite persons. They are the wyrd-principle given form, and they precede the Norse pantheon in mythological chronology. This is as close as the pre-theistic worldview comes to metaphysics: an acknowledgment that the world has structure, and that nobody designed it.

Chapter XV

Sacred Time & the Calendar

Before writing, before agriculture, before cities, people were counting. The calendar is among the oldest human technologies — and it was never merely practical. To track time was to know when the world required attention.

Someone had to watch the horizon for a lifetime so that everyone would know when to begin.

On calendrical authority
10,000
Years BCE — approximate date of the Warren Field pit alignment, the earliest known calendrical monument

The Problem of Time

Three Clocks That Do Not Agree

Any society attempting to track time faces an arithmetic problem that has no clean solution. Three natural cycles are available, and none divides evenly into another.

The day is unambiguous. The lunar month runs about 29.53 days — not a whole number. The solar year runs about 365.24 days, which is neither a whole number of days nor a whole number of lunar months. Twelve lunar months total roughly 354 days, falling eleven days short of the solar year.

This mismatch is the engine of calendrical history. A purely lunar calendar drifts through the seasons, completing a full cycle every 33 years — workable for ritual timing but useless for agriculture. A purely solar calendar loses the moon, which is the most visible and easily counted marker available to a pre-literate people.

Every sophisticated ancient calendar is an attempt to reconcile the two, usually by intercalation: inserting an extra month periodically to drag the lunar count back into alignment with the sun. Doing this correctly requires sustained observation across generations — and someone whose responsibility it is.

Deep Antiquity

The Earliest Counting

Tally Objects

Marked bones from the Upper Paleolithic have been proposed as lunar tallies. The best known is a bone plaque from the Abri Blanchard in France, around 30,000 BCE, bearing a serpentine arrangement of 69 marks. Alexander Marshack argued in The Roots of Civilization (1972) that the marks record lunar phases across roughly two months, with changes in the shape and orientation of each mark corresponding to changes in the moon's appearance.

Marshack's thesis was influential and remains contested. Critics note that the marks can be grouped in multiple ways, that 69 is not obviously lunar, and that microscopic analysis of tool changes — his key evidence for separate marking episodes — has proved difficult to replicate. The Ishango bone from the Congo, around 20,000 BCE, has attracted similar claims and similar objections.

The honest position is that Paleolithic peoples plainly made systematic notational marks, that lunar counting is a plausible use for them, and that no specific interpretation of any individual object commands agreement. This is a step-three claim in the terms of Chapter II: symbolic behaviour is secure, content is not.

Warren Field

The strongest early evidence is architectural rather than portable. At Warren Field in Aberdeenshire, Scotland, excavation revealed a line of twelve pits, dug around 8000 BCE and maintained for several centuries.

Analysis published by Vincent Gaffney and colleagues in 2013 argued that the pits vary in size and shape in a pattern corresponding to lunar phases, that the arc represents a lunar year of twelve months, and that the alignment of the whole feature points toward the midwinter solstice sunrise in a notch on the south-eastern horizon.

If the reading holds, Warren Field solves the reconciliation problem elegantly: twelve lunar months tracked in sequence, with an annual solar reset at the solstice to prevent drift. It would be the earliest known calendrical monument anywhere, predating Mesopotamian formal calendars by roughly five thousand years — and it was built by hunter-gatherers, not farmers.

Monuments

Architecture That Keeps Time

From the Neolithic onward, calendrical knowledge is embedded in buildings. A monument aligned to a solar event is a permanent, public, unfalsifiable instrument: it either admits the light on the right day or it does not.

c. 3,200 BCE

Newgrange, Ireland

The roof-box above the passage entrance admits sunrise light to the central chamber for about seventeen minutes at midwinter, and only then. Construction required prior knowledge of the solstice position and the precision to build to it.

c. 2,800 BCE

Maeshowe, Orkney

Aligned to midwinter sunset rather than sunrise — the setting sun illuminates the rear wall of the chamber. The same principle, inverted, suggesting deliberate variation rather than copied practice.

c. 2,500 BCE

Stonehenge, England

The principal axis addresses midsummer sunrise in one direction and midwinter sunset in the other. Isotope evidence from Durrington Walls shows feasting concentrated at midwinter, suggesting the winter alignment mattered most.

c. 1,600 BCE

Nebra Sky Disc, Germany

A bronze disc inlaid with gold showing sun or full moon, crescent, and a cluster widely identified as the Pleiades. The Pleiades' position relative to the moon may encode the rule for intercalating a thirteenth month — a portable calendrical instruction.

c. 200 BCE

Coligny Calendar, France

A bronze plate in Gaulish recording a five-year lunisolar cycle with intercalary months, and marking days as MAT or ANM — probably auspicious and inauspicious. The only substantial surviving Celtic calendar, and evidence that the eight-point festival year had formal structure.

Structure

The Eight-Point Year in Detail

The eight-station year described in Chapter XIV has an internal logic worth setting out. It is not an arbitrary division but the natural product of a specific method.

The two solstices are directly observable: the days on which the sun stops moving along the horizon and reverses. The equinoxes can be found by bisecting the interval between them. The four cross-quarter days are then found by bisecting again. Each subdivision requires only the ability to halve an interval, and the whole structure can be built from horizon observation with no arithmetic at all.

This explains why the eight-fold division recurs so widely: it is what you get when you repeatedly halve the solar year using only observation. The eight-spoked wheel found on Bronze Age European material is the diagram of that procedure.

Directly observed

The Solstices

Midwinter and midsummer. The sun's rising point halts and reverses along the horizon. Requires only a fixed observation position and a marked horizon — a stone, a notch, a post.

First bisection

The Equinoxes

Midway between the solstices, when day and night are equal and the sun rises due east. Can be found by counting or by bisecting the horizon arc between solstice extremes.

Second bisection

The Cross-Quarters

Imbolc, Beltane, Lughnasadh, Samhain — midway between each solstice and equinox. These track the agricultural and pastoral year more closely than the solar stations do.

Overlaid

The Lunar Count

Months run independently of the solar stations and must be periodically corrected. Festivals frequently specify both — the full moon nearest a given station — which is why their dates move.

Meaning

Why Timing Was Sacred

Ritual Must Be Punctual

In a cyclical cosmology, ritual is maintenance work. The solstice must be witnessed; the dead must be fed at the turning of the year; first fruits must be offered before the harvest is eaten. These acts are effective only if performed at the right moment, which makes accurate timekeeping a religious necessity rather than a convenience.

This creates a specific social role. Someone must watch the horizon across years, remember the sequence, and announce the date. Calendrical authority is among the oldest forms of religious authority, and it is unusually durable because it is verifiable: a calendar keeper who gets the solstice wrong is publicly wrong.

Time Is Not Uniform

Modern time is homogeneous — every hour equivalent to every other. Sacred time is not. Certain days are charged, dangerous, auspicious, or thin. The Coligny calendar's marking of days as MAT and ANM records exactly this: some days are fit for action and some are not.

Samhain and the midwinter period recur across northern Europe as intervals when the boundary between the living and the dead is permeable. This is not metaphor within the system. It is a statement about how the world behaves at particular points in the cycle, and it generates real behavioural rules about what may be done and when.

The Return, Not the Progress

Eliade's The Myth of the Eternal Return (1949) argued that archaic societies experience ritual as a re-entry into primordial time rather than a commemoration of it. The midwinter fire does not remember previous midwinters; it participates in the same moment, which recurs.

Eliade's framework has been criticized for flattening genuine cultural differences and for resting on a romantic reading of his sources. But the underlying observation holds for the material considered here: a calendar organized around recurrence rather than accumulation implies a cosmos that is maintained rather than one that is going somewhere.

Chapter XVI

The Festival Calendar

The previous chapter established how time was measured. This one sets out what was done with it — the named festivals of the Roman, Greek, Norse, Celtic, and Near Eastern worlds, and the structural patterns that recur across all of them.

For seven days the slave dined and the master served, and on the eighth day both knew exactly what they were again.

On Saturnalia

Orientation

What a Festival Is For

A festival is a scheduled interruption. Ordinary rules are suspended, ordinary work stops, and the community does something together that it does not do on other days. The scheduling is the point: everyone knows in advance, everyone participates, and the event recurs.

Most of the festivals in this chapter come from literate societies — Rome, Greece, Mesopotamia, and the Norse world through later Icelandic sources. This is a preservation artifact, not a claim that pre-literate peoples lacked festivals. The eight-station year described in Chapter XV is the reconstructed skeleton; what follows is what such a skeleton looks like when we can actually read the calendar.

A caution carried from Chapter II: Roman and Greek festivals are documented in sources written centuries after the practices began, often by authors who no longer understood the rites they described. Ovid guesses at etymologies. Plutarch offers three incompatible explanations for the same custom. The festivals are real; the explanations attached to them are frequently later reconstruction.

Rome

The Roman Year

The Roman calendar is the best-documented festival system of the ancient world. The fasti — calendars marking which days permitted public business — survive in inscribed fragments, and Ovid's Fasti narrates the festivals of the first six months in verse. Roughly a hundred days of the Roman year carried some religious designation.

15 February

Lupercalia

Among the oldest and strangest Roman rites, surviving until 494 CE. Goats and a dog were sacrificed at the Lupercal cave; young men wearing the skins ran a circuit striking bystanders with thongs cut from the hides. Women sought the blows, which were believed to promote fertility. The rite is pre-urban in character and the Romans themselves disagreed about its origin.

13–21 February

Parentalia & Feralia

Nine days honouring the family dead. Temples closed, magistrates set aside their insignia, and no marriages were performed. Families visited tombs with offerings of grain, salt, bread soaked in wine, and violets. The public closing rite on 21 February was the Feralia.

23 February

Terminalia

Honouring Terminus, the power of the boundary stone. Neighbours gathered at the marker dividing their land, garlanded it, and poured offerings into a pit beside it. The stone itself was the recipient — a survival of the object-power described in Chapter X.

15 March

Anna Perenna

A riverbank festival of drinking and improvised obscene song, at which participants prayed for as many years of life as cups they could drain. Popular rather than official, and one of the clearest surviving instances of licensed disorder in the Roman year.

28 April–3 May

Floralia

Games in honour of Flora, marked by loosened restrictions — coloured clothing instead of white, theatrical performances of notorious licentiousness, and the release of hares and goats. A fertility festival at the height of spring.

9, 11, 13 May

Lemuria

The counterpart to Parentalia: rites against the hostile dead. At midnight the head of household walked barefoot through the house spitting black beans over his shoulder, repeating a formula nine times without looking back. The lemures were expected to gather the beans and depart.

25 April

Robigalia

A procession to the fifth milestone on the Via Claudia, where a rust-coloured dog was sacrificed to avert grain rust from the crop. The recipient, Robigus, is not a god with mythology but the personified blight itself — a power addressed to keep it away.

17–23 December

Saturnalia

The most famous Roman festival, and the most thoroughly inverted. Courts and schools closed; the woollen bands binding Saturn's statue were untied; formal togas were exchanged for informal dress and the freedman's cap was worn by all. Gifts were exchanged — wax candles and small pottery figures among the traditional ones. Slaves were served by their masters and permitted licence of speech, and a mock king, the Saturnalicius princeps, presided over the household with authority to issue absurd commands.

Saturnalia and the Logic of Inversion

Saturnalia deserves separate treatment because it is the clearest ancient example of a pattern that recurs worldwide: the festival of reversal.

Its stated rationale was a return to the Golden Age under Saturn, when no one served and no one owned. For seven days the social order was suspended rather than merely relaxed. The inversion was structured and temporary, and everyone knew when it would end.

Anthropologists have read such festivals in two competing ways. The safety-valve reading, associated with Max Gluckman, holds that licensed reversal discharges resentment that would otherwise threaten the order — the ritual permits rebellion in order to prevent it. Victor Turner's reading, developed from his work on liminality, treats the inversion as genuine communitas: a temporary experience of undifferentiated humanity that renews the structure by stepping outside it.

Both readings assume the inversion is contained. What makes Saturnalia notable is how explicitly the containment was built in: a fixed duration, a mock king with no real power, and a return to normality on a known date.

For seven days the slave dined and the master served, and on the eighth day both knew exactly what they were again.

— On the structure of ritual inversion

Greece

The Athenian Year

Athenian festivals were organized around the lunar month, with the calendar named for the festivals themselves — the month of Anthesterion contains the Anthesteria. Because the lunar year required periodic correction, festivals moved relative to the seasons and intercalary months were inserted by decree, sometimes for political convenience.

Pyanepsion · Autumn

Thesmophoria

The most widespread festival in the Greek world, conducted by married women and closed to men. Pigs thrown into underground chambers at an earlier point in the year were retrieved decayed, placed on altars, and mixed with seed grain. Three days: Ascent, Fasting, and Fair Offspring. Pure chthonic practice, discussed in Chapter XIII.

Anthesterion · Feb–Mar

Anthesteria

A three-day wine festival that doubled as a festival of the dead. Day one opened the new wine; day two involved drinking contests and a rite in which the wife of the archon was ceremonially married to Dionysus; day three fed the dead with a pot of cooked grain. Doorways were smeared with pitch and buckthorn chewed to ward off spirits, and the festival closed with the cry that the dead should now depart.

Boedromion · Autumn

Eleusinian Mysteries

The most important initiatory cult of the ancient world, running for roughly two millennia. Initiates processed the fourteen miles from Athens to Eleusis, fasted, drank the kykeon, and witnessed something in the Telesterion that they were forbidden to describe. The secret held remarkably well — we still do not know what was shown.

Hekatombaion · Summer

Panathenaia

The civic festival of Athens, held annually and in expanded form every fourth year. A new woven peplos was carried in procession to clothe the ancient olive-wood statue of Athena. Games, recitation of Homer, and a hecatomb — a hundred-ox sacrifice whose meat was distributed to the citizen body.

Elaphebolion · Spring

City Dionysia

The festival at which Athenian tragedy and comedy were performed in competition. Nearly every surviving Greek play was written for it. Religious observance and dramatic art were not separate categories: the theatre was a sanctuary and the performance an offering.

Karneios · Summer

Karneia (Sparta)

A nine-day Dorian festival during which military campaigning was prohibited. Sparta's delayed arrival at Marathon and its token force at Thermopylae were both attributed to the Karneia — a festival calendar with direct strategic consequences.

The Sacred Truce

The Olympic ekecheiria — usually rendered as truce, more literally a holding of hands — suspended hostilities to allow safe travel to and from the games. It did not end wars, and it was violated on occasion, but it created a recognized period during which armed interference with pilgrims was a sacrilege prosecutable by the sanctuary.

This is festival time functioning as a legal instrument. The calendar generated a zone of protection that no political authority had created and none could unilaterally revoke.

The North

Norse and Germanic Observance

Norse festival evidence is thinner and later than the classical material. The principal sources — Snorri Sturluson's Heimskringla, the Icelandic sagas, and scattered Anglo-Saxon references — were written by Christians two or more centuries after conversion. What they preserve is real but filtered.

The organizing term is blót, a sacrificial feast. Snorri describes three principal annual blót: one at winter nights for a good season, one at midwinter for growth, and one in summer for victory. Blood from the sacrificed animals was sprinkled on participants and on the walls of the hall with a twig; the meat was boiled and eaten communally, with toasts drunk in sequence.

Mid-October

Vetrnætr — Winter Nights

The start of the Norse winter half-year. Associated with the álfablót, a household rite for the elves conducted privately — one saga records a traveller being refused shelter because the rite was underway and strangers were not permitted.

Midwinter

Jól — Yule

The midwinter feast, later fixed to Christmas by royal decree in Norway. Associated with heavy drinking, oath-swearing over a boar, and the Wild Hunt — the belief that the dead ride abroad on winter nights. Bede records the Anglo-Saxon Môdranith, Mothers' Night, at the same season.

Early February

Dísablót & Disting

A sacrifice to the dísir, female ancestral or tutelary powers. At Uppsala it coincided with the Disting, a great assembly and market — law-court, fair, and religious rite in one gathering.

Every ninth year

The Uppsala Sacrifice

Adam of Bremen, writing around 1070, describes a nine-yearly festival at Uppsala at which nine males of each species, including men, were hung in a sacred grove. Adam never visited and was hostile; the account may be exaggerated. Archaeology at Old Uppsala confirms a major cult site but not the specifics.

The Anglo-Saxon evidence is slighter still. Bede's De temporum ratione (725 CE) names months including Eosturmonath and Hrethmonath, attributing them to goddesses Éostre and Hretha. Bede is the only source for either, writing over a century after conversion, and scholars remain divided over whether he recorded genuine tradition or supplied plausible etymologies for month-names he no longer understood.

The Near East

Akitu and the New Year

Mesopotamian festival practice is documented earlier than any other, in cuneiform ritual texts from the third millennium BCE onward. The most important was Akitu, the New Year festival, celebrated over eleven days at the spring equinox.

The Babylonian version included an episode of great interest for the study of kingship. On the fifth day the king was brought before the statue of Marduk, stripped of his regalia, and struck on the cheek by the high priest. He then knelt and declared that he had not sinned against the city, neglected its temples, or struck its citizens. If tears came at the second blow, the god was held to be favourable. The regalia were then restored.

Kingship was renewed annually by being suspended annually. The parallel with Saturnalia's mock king is structural rather than historical — two unconnected cultures arriving at the same device of temporary reversal to reinforce a permanent order.

The Persian Nowruz, still observed, marks the spring equinox and is attested in Achaemenid-period contexts. Its continuity into the present makes it plausibly the longest continuously celebrated festival in the world.

Egyptian festivals were tied to the Nile rather than the solar year. Wepet-Renpet, the Opening of the Year, coincided with the inundation; the Opet festival at Thebes carried the divine images in procession between Karnak and Luxor over a period that expanded from eleven to twenty-seven days.

Patterns

What Recurs Across All of Them

Set side by side, these calendars share structural features that no cultural contact explains. The recurrence suggests that festivals solve a small number of consistent social problems.

Pattern 1

Feeding the Dead

Parentalia, Lemuria, Anthesteria, Samhain, Vetrnætr. Every calendar surveyed sets aside a period when the dead approach and must be fed, then formally dismissed. Frequently at the year's boundary.

Pattern 2

Licensed Inversion

Saturnalia, Akitu, Anna Perenna, Floralia. Rank suspended, obscenity permitted, mock authority installed. Always bounded by a known end date.

Pattern 3

First Fruits & Firstlings

Lughnasadh, the Athenian Thargelia, Roman harvest rites. Nothing consumed until a portion is surrendered — the reciprocity principle of Chapter XIV given a date.

Pattern 4

Purification & Expulsion

Lupercalia, Lemuria, the Greek Thargelia with its pharmakos scapegoat driven out. Accumulated pollution is periodically removed from the community wholesale rather than individually.

Pattern 5

Truce & Suspended Conflict

The Olympic ekecheiria, the Karneia, the Roman feriae on which legal business halted. Festival time creates protected intervals that political authority does not control.

Pattern 6

Renewal of Authority

Akitu's humiliation of the king, the Saturnalicius princeps, Norse oath-swearing at Yule. Power is periodically returned to its source and reissued rather than simply held.

Pattern 7

Assembly & Exchange

Disting, Lughnasadh, the Panathenaia. Festivals concentrate dispersed populations, and where people gather, law is spoken, disputes are settled, goods are traded, and marriages are arranged.

Pattern 8

Initiation & Revelation

Eleusis above all, but the pattern is broad: a fixed date at which a cohort is brought across a threshold together, having fasted and processed and been shown something reserved for them.

Afterlife

What Happened to These Festivals

Festivals proved considerably more durable than the theologies attached to them, which is the central observation of Chapter XXI in miniature. The Christianization of Europe rarely abolished a festival outright; it more often renamed and rescheduled it.

Pope Gregory I's letter to Abbot Mellitus in 601 CE states the policy explicitly: the temples should not be destroyed but converted, and since the people are accustomed to slaughtering oxen in sacrifice, some solemnity should be substituted on the same day so that they may continue to gather and feast while directing it elsewhere.

The results are visible throughout the modern calendar. Samhain became All Saints and Hallowe'en. Midwinter observance became Christmas, retaining the evergreens, the log, the lights, and the gift exchange — the last plausibly descended from Saturnalian sigillaria. Imbolc became Candlemas and, in the folk register, Groundhog Day. Lupercalia's February slot is now occupied by a saint's day associated with courtship.

This is not evidence of a pagan survival in disguise, a claim frequently overstated. It is evidence of something more prosaic and more interesting: the timing and the social function of the festival were harder to displace than its explanation. People kept gathering when they had always gathered, and doing largely what they had always done. The account of why changed around them.

Chapter XVI

Women, Gender & Ritual Authority

Who was permitted to act on behalf of the community? The evidence is fragmentary, heavily mediated by male observers, and has been made to support conclusions it cannot bear — in both directions. This chapter tries to say what can actually be established.

Thirty thousand years of carved women, and we cannot say what one of them was for.

On the figurines

The Problem

Two Bad Answers

The study of gender in prehistoric religion has been distorted by two opposing errors, each of which has had a long run.

The first is the assumption of universal patriarchy: that ritual authority everywhere and always belonged to men, and that female figures in the record represent objects of male attention rather than agents. This shaped a century of interpretation in which female figurines were read as fertility charms or erotica by default, and burials with weapons were assumed male regardless of skeletal evidence.

The second is the Mother Goddess hypothesis: that prehistoric Europe was a peaceful, matrifocal civilization worshipping a single Great Goddess, destroyed by patriarchal Indo-European invaders. This is more sympathetic and equally unsupported.

Both errors share a method — projecting a total social model onto thin evidence. What follows tries to stay closer to what the material actually shows, which is more varied and less tidy than either story.

The Gimbutas Controversy

The Mother Goddess and Its Critics

Marija Gimbutas (1921–1994) was a Lithuanian-American archaeologist of considerable standing whose early work on Indo-European origins — the Kurgan hypothesis, locating Proto-Indo-European speakers in the Pontic steppe — has been substantially vindicated by recent ancient DNA studies.

Her later work made a much larger claim. In The Goddesses and Gods of Old Europe (1974) and The Language of the Goddess (1989), Gimbutas argued that Neolithic southeastern Europe constituted a unified civilization — matrilineal, egalitarian, peaceful, and centred on the worship of a Great Goddess whose symbolism she read across thousands of artifacts.

The archaeological reception was largely negative, and the objections are substantive rather than merely conservative:

The symbolic decoding is unconstrained. Gimbutas interpreted chevrons, spirals, meanders, and Vs as goddess symbols across a range of contexts, with no independent method for testing whether a given mark was symbolic at all. Nearly any decorated object could be enrolled.

Figurines are not necessarily deities. Peter Ucko's comparative work demonstrated that figurines in documented societies serve many purposes — toys, teaching aids, initiation objects, portraits, dolls, magical instruments. Assuming religious function is an inference, not an observation.

Matrilineality does not follow from female imagery. Societies with prominent goddesses are not reliably better for women; classical Athens venerated Athena while restricting women severely. The inference from iconography to social structure does not hold.

The peaceful Old Europe is contradicted. Neolithic sites across the region show fortification, weapon trauma, and massacre deposits — Talheim and Asparn-Schletz being the starkest examples.

Gimbutas remains widely read outside archaeology and her influence on modern paganism is considerable. The distinction worth holding is between her steppe-origins work, which was ahead of its time, and her goddess synthesis, which the evidence does not support.

The Figurines

What the Venus Figures Actually Show

Upper Paleolithic female figurines — the so-called Venus figures — span roughly 40,000 to 10,000 BCE and a geographic range from the Pyrenees to Siberia. The Venus of Hohle Fels, at around 40,000 BCE, is the oldest known figurative sculpture of a human being.

Interpretations have included fertility idols, goddess images, erotica, self-portraits made by women viewing their own bodies from above, teaching objects about pregnancy and childbirth, and markers of exchange networks. All are proposals; none is established.

What can be said with more confidence is narrower and still interesting. The figures are overwhelmingly female. They are frequently portable, small enough to be carried. Facial features are usually absent or minimal while bodily features are emphasized. Many were deliberately broken or deposited rather than casually lost. And the tradition persisted for roughly thirty thousand years across a continent — a duration and distribution that argues against any purely local explanation.

Thirty thousand years of small carved women, and we cannot say what a single one was for. That is the honest position, and it is worth stating plainly before offering any theory.

— On the interpretation of Paleolithic figurines

Evidence

Women in Documented Ritual Roles

Where evidence is better — burials with clear grave assemblages, or historical periods with written sources — women appear in ritual roles regularly and in some cases predominantly.

c. 12,000 BCE

The Hilazon Tachtit Burial

An elderly Natufian woman interred with fifty tortoise shells, a leopard's pelvis, an eagle's wing, a boar's foreleg, and a human foot. Published as the earliest identified shaman burial. The oldest individual we can plausibly name as a ritual specialist is a woman.

c. 1,600 BCE

Minoan Priestesses

Cretan seal art and frescoes repeatedly show women in what appear to be officiating roles — before altars, at tree shrines, handling snakes, receiving processions. Male figures appear more often as participants than as officiants.

Classical Greece

The Thesmophoria

The most widespread festival in the Greek world was conducted by women, closed to men, and involved retrieving decayed offerings from underground chambers to mix with seed grain. Men were excluded from a central agricultural rite of the polis.

Viking Age

The Völva

Norse sources describe female practitioners of seiðr — prophecy and spirit-work. Over forty graves across Scandinavia contain iron staffs of a type matching literary descriptions, most of them female burials. Text and archaeology converge unusually well here.

Rome

The Vestals

Six women maintained the civic hearth-fire of Rome. They were legally independent of male guardianship, could give evidence without oath, and their ritual failure was treated as a threat to the state itself.

Delphi

The Pythia

The most authoritative oracle in the Greek world spoke through a woman. Male priests attended and may have transcribed, but the oracular voice itself was female, and states consulted it before war and colonization.

Germanic

Seeresses in Roman Sources

Tacitus names Veleda of the Bructeri, whose judgments carried decisive weight in war and diplomacy, and notes that the Germans regarded certain women as possessing prophetic power. Roman writers found this striking enough to record repeatedly.

Birka, Sweden

Grave Bj 581

A high-status Viking burial with full weapon assemblage and gaming pieces, assumed male for over a century. Genomic analysis in 2017 identified the individual as female, prompting substantial reassessment of how burial assemblages had been sexed by assumption.

Patterns

What the Distribution Suggests

Domains, Not Exclusion

The evidence rarely shows women excluded from ritual life. It more often shows ritual life divided into domains, with different access rules. Women's ritual authority clusters around birth, death, agricultural fertility, prophecy, healing, and the domestic hearth — and in several documented cases these were not peripheral but central to communal survival.

Secrecy Cuts Both Ways

Women's rites are frequently closed to men, as men's are to women. The Thesmophoria excluded men entirely, and our knowledge of it is correspondingly poor — the sources are male writers reporting what they were not permitted to see. Some of the apparent thinness of the record for women's ritual is an artifact of who was allowed to observe and who was allowed to write.

Third Categories

Several traditions record ritual specialists occupying positions outside a simple binary. Norse seiðr carried associations of ergi — unmanliness — which made its practice by men socially fraught while Odin himself was said to practise it. Siberian and North American ethnography documents specialists whose ritual role involved taking on gender-crossing identity. The pattern suggests that liminal social position and ritual capacity were sometimes understood as connected.

Assumption Has Shaped the Data

The Birka case is the clearest warning available. A burial was sexed male for over a century on the basis of its contents rather than its skeleton, and that determination then circulated as evidence about who bore weapons. Osteological and genomic re-examination of museum collections has repeatedly overturned such assignments. Some of what the record appeared to show about gender was an artifact of what excavators expected.

Chapter IX

Regional Traditions

The animist, shamanic, and chthonic worldview expressed itself differently across different landscapes, ecologies, and cultures — but the underlying grammar remained recognizable from the Orkney archipelago to the banks of the Jordan, from the Siberian taiga to the Anatolian plateau.

Before agriculture, before pottery, before writing — they moved twenty tonnes of stone and carved foxes on it.

On Göbekli Tepe

The Deep Past

Paleolithic Europe: The Cave Tradition (c. 40,000–10,000 BCE)

The cave art tradition of Upper Paleolithic Europe represents the oldest continuous body of religious expression in the world. From the Cantabrian coast of Spain (Altamira, El Castillo, Tito Bustillo) through the Dordogne and Ariège regions of France (Lascaux, Chauvet, Font-de-Gaume, Niaux, Trois-Frères) to the caves of southern Germany (Vogelherd, Geissenklösterle, Hohle Fels), a recognizable tradition persisted for approximately 30,000 years — longer than any subsequent religious tradition in human history.

The Trois-Frères "Sorcerer"

Among the most important images in this tradition is the so-called "Sorcerer" of the Trois-Frères cave in Ariège, France: a therianthropic figure depicted in the deepest chamber of the cave, combining antler-horns, owl eyes, a horse's tail, and human legs. The figure appears to be dancing or moving. The interpretation of the great French prehistorian Henri Breuil — that this is a shamanic figure, a "master of animals" overseeing the animal spirit representations around it — has been repeatedly challenged and repeatedly defended. Whether it depicts a costumed human, a spirit being, or a shaman's vision of themselves in trance, it embodies the animist-shamanic worldview with extraordinary clarity: human and animal identity interpenetrate in the person of the ritual specialist.

Resonant Spaces

Acoustics researcher Iegor Reznikoff (with Michel Dauvois) published a landmark study in 1988 (Bulletin de la Société Préhistorique Française, 85:238–246) demonstrating that the placement of paintings in Paleolithic caves correlates significantly with acoustic resonance points — locations where the human voice produces extraordinary reverberant effects. Chambers with painting concentrate at spots where drumming and chanting would produce the most dramatic sonic effects. This argues strongly for a ritual, trance-ceremony use of the caves rather than simple mark-making.

Anatolia

Göbekli Tepe (c. 9,600–8,000 BCE)

The site of Göbekli Tepe in southeast Turkey (excavated by Klaus Schmidt from 1994 until his death in 2014) overturned one of the central assumptions of prehistoric anthropology: that monumental architecture requires settled agricultural societies. Göbekli Tepe's massive limestone T-pillars — the largest over 5.5 meters tall, weighing 10–20 tonnes — were constructed by hunter-gatherer peoples approximately 6,000 years before Stonehenge and 7,000 years before the Egyptian pyramids.

The site comprises at least 20 circular and oval enclosures, of which only a fraction have been excavated. The T-pillars are carved with high-relief animals: foxes, aurochs, gazelles, snakes, ducks, cranes, scorpions, and — most remarkably — vultures. Some pillars bear abstract symbols (H-shapes, crescents) whose meaning remains unknown.

What the Animals Mean

No deity iconography has been identified at Göbekli Tepe. No human figures dominate the carvings; no creation narrative can be associated with the site. The animals are themselves the content. The most likely interpretation, given the totemic and animist parallels from other cultures, is that different enclosures were associated with different clan groups, each identified by its animal — the fox enclosure, the vulture enclosure, the serpent enclosure.

The vulture is particularly significant: vultures consume the dead, connecting them to the underworld. Platforms at the site may have been used for sky burial — the exposure of the dead for consumption by vultures before bone collection. The connection between vulture imagery and the processing of the dead appears also at Çatalhöyük, suggesting a regional cultural practice of transforming the dead through the agency of the sky-realm scavenger.

"Göbekli Tepe changes everything. Monumental religious architecture precedes agriculture. It may have caused it — the organizational demand of building and maintaining the great sanctuary may have been the pressure that drove the adoption of farming as a reliable food supply for the pilgrims and builders."

— Klaus Schmidt, Sie bauten die ersten Tempel (2006); paraphrase

The Fertile Crescent

Çatalhöyük (c. 7,500–5,700 BCE)

The Neolithic settlement of Çatalhöyük in south-central Anatolia was among the world's largest and earliest towns — a dense cluster of mud-brick houses housing an estimated 3,000–8,000 people over some 2,000 years. It provides our richest window into the domestic religious life of early agricultural society, and its religion is unmistakably non-theistic.

The Dead Beneath the Floor

The most striking feature of Çatalhöyük's religion is the burial of the dead directly beneath the plastered floors of the houses — sometimes beneath sleeping platforms, meaning the living literally slept above their dead. Over 400 burials have been found in floor and platform contexts. The dead were often placed in the fetal position, suggesting a connection between burial and the process of birth: the earth below as the womb from which the dead might be reborn.

Skulls were occasionally removed and re-plastered (like the PPNB examples, see Chapter III), suggesting continued engagement with specific ancestors after initial burial. Some skulls circulated through the community — passed between households — before final deposition, indicating that ancestral power was a community resource, not private property.

The Art: Aurochs and Vultures

House walls at Çatalhöyük were painted, replastered, and repainted — accumulating hundreds of layers over the occupation period. The imagery includes large aurochs (wild cattle) with hunting scenes, vultures with headless human figures, handprints, and geometric patterns. The aurochs skulls were built into the walls as installations.

The vulture-and-headless-human scenes are now interpreted as sky burial imagery — the dead being processed by vultures, a common practice in the Near East that continued into historical times (Persian and Zoroastrian practice). No deity receives the dead; the vulture transforms them.

Atlantic Europe

The Megalith Builders (c. 4,500–2,000 BCE)

The megalithic tradition of Atlantic Europe — from Portugal through Spain, France, Britain, Ireland, and Scandinavia — produced the most architecturally impressive body of non-theistic religious building in the world. The tradition spans approximately 2,500 years and includes tens of thousands of monuments: passage tombs, gallery graves, portal dolmens, stone circles, standing stone alignments, and cairns.

Stonehenge: Revised Understanding

Stonehenge has been subjected to more interpretive revision than any prehistoric monument. Current understanding (following recent excavations by the Stonehenge Riverside Project and the work of Mike Parker Pearson) sees Stonehenge as one node in a larger sacred landscape organized around the themes of the living and the dead, aligned to the solstice sunrise and sunset.

The neighboring timber monument at Durrington Walls (2,500 BCE) was the settlement of the living; Stonehenge itself was the domain of the dead — its cremation deposits (the largest Neolithic cremation cemetery yet found in Britain) date throughout its use period. A ceremonial avenue connected the two monuments to the River Avon, and the entire complex is now understood as a processional landscape: the living celebrated midsummer at Durrington Walls, moved to Stonehenge for midwinter, and the river carried the dead between the two worlds.

No deity is named in the construction of Stonehenge. The powers engaged are astronomical (solstice cycles), ancestral (the cremated dead), and geographical (the river as boundary between life and death). This is pure non-theistic sacred geography.

Carnac, Brittany (c. 4,700–3,300 BCE)

The Carnac alignments in Brittany are the world's largest collection of standing stones — over 3,000 menhirs arranged in parallel rows extending for several kilometers. Their function remains disputed, but astronomical alignment (including lunar standstill markers, proposed by Alexander Thom in his systematic surveys of 1971) and territorial marking are the most plausible explanations. No mythological framework survives; the stones themselves are the enduring record of a community's sustained engagement with the landscape over centuries.

Pre-Olympian Aegean

Minoan and Mycenaean Religion (c. 3,000–1,200 BCE)

Before the Homeric pantheon crystallized into the canonical Olympian twelve, the Aegean world practiced a religion that scholars still struggle to describe in the absence of decipherable texts. Linear A (Minoan) remains undeciphered; Linear B (Mycenaean Greek) provides lists of deity offerings but no mythology. What the archaeology reveals is a religious world organized around sacred peaks, sacred caves, sacred trees, pillar cults, and a female figure associated with snakes, birds, and nature — none of it yet consolidated into the personal, narrative, biographically complex gods of Homer.

The Minoan Tree Cult

Minoan glyptic art (seal stones and gold rings) repeatedly depicts scenes of female figures ecstatically dancing before or with sacred trees, with deities descending from above or emerging from the earth. These scenes have been interpreted (by Nanno Marinatos and others) as rituals of epiphany — moments when sacred power manifests through the tree. The tree is not a symbol; it is a site of divine presence that can be activated by the right ritual approach.

Pillar Crypts

Many Minoan palaces include "pillar crypts" — small, dark basement rooms centered on a single large stone pillar, with evidence of liquid offerings (troughs, channels) at the pillar's base. The pillar appears to have concentrated sacred power in the same way a baetylus or standing stone does: not as a representation of a god but as the power itself, in condensed form. This is fetish religion in an architectural context.

Northern Europe

Pre-Celtic and Pre-Norse Religion of Northern Europe

Before the distinctive mythological systems of Celtic and Norse religion emerged in their historically documented forms, the peoples of northern Europe practiced a religion that can be partially reconstructed from archaeology, placename evidence, and survivals in later folk practice. This proto-religion was, in structure, non-theistic: it featured land-wights (landvætter in Old Norse), sacred places without named divine owners, and ancestor veneration centered on burial mounds.

The Vætter: Land Spirits

The Old Norse landvætter ("land-wights," spirit powers of the land) represent the pre-theistic layer of Scandinavian religion more clearly than any other concept. These are not gods — they have no mythology, no names in most cases, no genealogies. They are simply the inherent spiritual presence of specific landscapes: the spirit of this particular hill, this particular fjord, this particular forest.

The Icelandic sagas describe incoming settlers performing rituals to appease or win over the landvætter before occupying a new piece of land. The Landnámabók (Book of Settlements, c. 1200) preserves numerous examples. Critically, this ritual predates and exists alongside the worship of Odin, Thor, and Freyr — it is a distinct, older layer that the theistic pantheon never displaced.

Bog Bodies: Sacrifice to the Land

The bog bodies of northern Europe — over 1,000 individuals preserved by the anaerobic conditions of peat bogs in Denmark, Britain, Ireland, Germany, and the Netherlands — include many who show signs of ritual killing: garroting, decapitation, repeated wounding, and deliberate placement in the bog. The most famous, Tollund Man (Denmark, c. 400 BCE) and Lindow Man (England, c. 100 BCE), were killed by multiple methods (the "triple death" of garroting, skull trauma, and throat-cutting) and deposited in the watery liminal boundary between land and water.

The bog itself — a place that is neither solid land nor open water, that neither fully preserves nor fully decomposes, that is neither upper world nor lower world but something in between — was a natural sacred boundary. Offerings into bogs include vast quantities of weapons, food vessels, jewelry, and animal bones alongside human bodies. The bog received these gifts in its own right, not on behalf of any named deity.

Chapter XVIII

Beyond Europe: Comparative Traditions

Everything so far has been overwhelmingly European. That is a limitation of the evidence and of the scholarship, not a claim about where religion happened. This chapter asks whether the framework survives contact with traditions it was not built from.

The categories were built in Europe. That they fit elsewhere may say more about the categories than the elsewhere.

On comparative method

The Bias

Why This Book Has Been Eurocentric

The concentration on Europe and the Near East in the preceding chapters has causes worth naming, because they shape what the evidence can support.

European prehistory has been excavated more intensively, for longer, and with more funding than any comparable region. The categories used throughout this book — animism, shamanism, totemism, taboo — were coined by European scholars, largely in the nineteenth century, and frequently in colonial contexts where the peoples being described had no say in the description.

This creates a real methodological hazard. If the categories were built from European material and then applied elsewhere, finding that they fit elsewhere may reflect the categories' elasticity rather than any genuine similarity. The test of a framework is whether it illuminates cases it was not designed around, and whether it can register differences rather than absorbing everything into itself.

A second point of respect matters here. Several traditions discussed below are living religions with current practitioners, not archaeological remains. They are not evidence about the past frozen for inspection; they are ongoing.

Siberia

The Source Case

Siberian practice deserves first place because the word shaman comes from it, and because much of the general theory was extrapolated from a small ethnographic base.

The Evenki, Buryat, Yakut, Chukchi, Nenets and neighbouring peoples maintained practices involving a specialist who entered trance to travel among spirits on the community's behalf. The classic apparatus — drum, spirit-costume with iron pendants, animal helpers, a three-tiered cosmos joined by a world tree or pole — is genuinely attested.

But the ethnography also shows variation that Eliade's synthesis smoothed away. Some groups distinguish white and black practitioners with entirely separate functions. Some hold the role hereditary, others by election through illness. Some traditions emphasize soul-flight, others possession — a distinction Eliade treated as fundamental but which the field data blurs.

Two further points bear on the argument of this book. The Siberian cosmos includes named powers — masters of animal species, spirits of specific places, and in some traditions a remote sky being — which sit awkwardly on any strict theistic / non-theistic divide. And these traditions were documented largely under Russian imperial and then Soviet pressure, including active suppression of practitioners. What was recorded is a tradition already under severe stress.

Australia

The Dreaming and the Deepest Continuity

Aboriginal Australian traditions represent the longest continuous religious cultures known, with occupation of the continent securely dated beyond 50,000 years and some sites, such as Madjedbebe, pushing toward 65,000.

The concept usually rendered as the Dreaming — Tjukurpa in Pitjantjatjara, Altyerre in Arrernte, with many other regional terms — does not translate cleanly. It refers simultaneously to the formative period in which ancestral beings shaped the land, to the ongoing reality those beings constitute, to the Law derived from their actions, and to the songs, designs, and ceremonies that maintain the relationship.

Several features connect directly to earlier chapters. Country is not scenery but a network of specific sites, each associated with particular ancestral beings and particular responsibilities — sacred geography of exactly the kind described in Chapter VI, but systematized far beyond anything recoverable from European prehistory. Songlines encode routes across the landscape in sung sequence, functioning simultaneously as navigation, history, and liturgy. Totemic affiliation regulates marriage and imposes species responsibilities, as described in Chapter X.

Two cautions. Australian traditions are enormously diverse across hundreds of language groups, and generalizing across them repeats the flattening error. And much ceremonial knowledge is restricted — by gender, by initiation grade, by kin group. Published ethnography frequently contains material that should not have been recorded, and this remains a live grievance rather than a historical one.

The Americas

Continuity, Rupture, and Recovery

Indigenous American traditions span an entire hemisphere and resist summary. Three observations bear on this book's framework.

Reciprocity is explicit and elaborated. The obligation to give back for what is taken — from an animal, a plant, a river — is stated directly across many traditions, in forms considerably more articulate than anything inferable from European archaeology. Robin Wall Kimmerer's account of the Honourable Harvest in Braiding Sweetgrass (2013) sets out a working ethic that the do ut des principle of Chapter XIV only gestures at.

Animacy can be grammatical. In Potawatomi and related Algonquian languages, nouns are marked animate or inanimate, and the animate class includes plants, stones, and water in ways that do not map onto biological categories. This is the strongest available counter to the assumption that animism is a belief people hold. In these languages it is a feature of how statements are constructed — closer to grammar than to doctrine.

Monumental construction without states. Poverty Point in Louisiana, built around 1700 BCE by hunter-gatherers, comprises earthworks moving roughly 750,000 cubic metres of soil. Watson Brake is older still, around 3500 BCE. As at Göbekli Tepe, monumental religious architecture precedes agriculture and hierarchy — independent confirmation on another continent.

The rupture must also be stated. European contact produced catastrophic population collapse and deliberate suppression of religious practice, including legal prohibition of ceremonies into the twentieth century in both the United States and Canada. Many traditions were broken; others were maintained covertly; many are being actively recovered. What ethnographers recorded was frequently the aftermath of destruction, mistaken for a baseline.

Africa

Ancestors, Divination, and the Longest Record

Sub-Saharan Africa holds the deepest archaeological record of symbolic behaviour anywhere. The Blombos Cave ochre engravings from around 77,000 BCE, the Pinnacle Point pigment processing from around 100,000 BCE, and the Border Cave burials establish that the cognitive capacities underlying religion were present in Africa long before their appearance in Europe.

Among living traditions, two features connect to earlier chapters with particular force.

Ancestor relations are the structural core. Across an enormous range of societies, the dead remain participants: consulted before decisions, propitiated during illness, named in libation, and treated as holding continuing authority within the lineage. The pattern described in Chapters IV and V appears here in full working order rather than as inference from graves.

Divination is formalized and central. Yoruba Ifá divination operates a corpus of 256 odu, each associated with a body of verses that the diviner must memorize and apply interpretively. This is not a simple yes-or-no oracle but a hermeneutic system requiring years of training — and it demonstrates that the practices surveyed in Chapter XI could reach considerable intellectual sophistication.

Evans-Pritchard's Witchcraft, Oracles and Magic among the Azande (1937) remains the classic demonstration that such systems are internally rational: within its premises, Azande reasoning about misfortune is coherent, self-correcting, and no more circular than many Western explanatory frameworks.

The Arctic

Inuit Traditions and the Master of Animals

Inuit and Yupik traditions across Greenland, Arctic Canada, Alaska, and Chukotka developed in an environment where survival depended on hunting large marine mammals, and their religious structure reflects that dependence precisely.

The figure recorded as Sedna, Nuliajuk, or Takannaaluk — names vary by region — is described as dwelling at the sea bottom and holding the marine animals. When taboos are broken, the transgressions gather in her hair, which she cannot comb, and she withholds the animals. The angakkuq travels to her, combs her hair, and the hunting resumes.

This is instructive for the theism question. Sedna has a name, a biography, and intentions — she is not an impersonal power. But she is not a creator, is not worshipped, and does not issue commandments. The relationship is transactional and remedial: something has gone wrong, and a specialist repairs it. This sits between the categories this book has been using, and the fit is genuinely imperfect.

The structural connection to Chapter IX is direct. Taboo violations cause the animals to withdraw; ritual restores the relationship. The moral logic is ecological rather than juridical — the consequence of transgression is scarcity, not punishment.

Assessment

Does the Framework Hold?

Testing the book's categories against this material yields a mixed verdict, which is the honest outcome.

Holds well

Relational Animism

The world as a community of persons, only some human, is not a European projection. It is stated explicitly by practitioners across every region surveyed, sometimes encoded in grammar itself.

Holds well

Reciprocity Ethics

Give-in-order-to-receive, and the obligation to return for what is taken, recur across unconnected traditions with remarkable consistency — frequently in more articulate form than the European evidence permits.

Holds partly

Shamanism

Trance specialists working on the community's behalf are genuinely widespread. But the tidy Eliadean template flattens real variation, and Kehoe's objection to using one Siberian word for all of it has force.

Strains

The Non-Theistic Category

Sedna, the animal masters, and the Australian ancestral beings are named, characterful, and intentional — yet not creators, not worshipped, not lawgivers. The binary this book relies on is too coarse for them.

That last finding is the most important. The distinction between non-theistic power and personified deity, which has done a great deal of work in this book, describes a continuum rather than a boundary. Many traditions sit permanently in the middle without progressing in either direction, which undermines any implication that non-theistic practice is an early stage that theism replaces.

The framework is therefore best treated as a set of useful distinctions rather than a developmental sequence. It illuminates real features of the European material. It should not be taken as a law of religious history, and the comparative evidence is what shows why.

Before the GodsXX. The Rise of Theism

Chapter X

The Rise of Theism

Named gods did not replace the old religion. They grew out of it — as local powers accumulated names, personalities, and mythologies; as states required legible, universal deities; as literacy fixed what had been fluid. The animist substrate never disappeared. It went underground.

First the power had no name. Then it had a name. Then it had a story, and a temple, and a priesthood.

On the theistic turn

The Process

How Animist Powers Become Gods

The transition from non-theistic nature religion to anthropomorphic polytheism was not an event or a discovery. It was a gradual process of crystallization that took different forms in different cultures and proceeded at different rates. Scholars debate its causes intensely, but several factors appear consistently across cases where the transition is archaeologically or historically traceable.

Step 1: Naming

The first step toward theism is the naming of a previously nameless power. The spring at Bath was simply a hot spring with power — then it became Sulis. The power of the sea was simply the sea — then it became Poseidon. The name creates a stable referent; it allows the power to be addressed consistently; it begins to give the power a kind of identity that transcends the specific place.

Step 2: Personification

Once named, a power tends to acquire personal qualities. It has a character — this deity is wrathful, that one is healing, another is associated with craftsmanship. Character implies biography: how did the deity become this way? What has happened to them? The first mythological narratives emerge to explain the deity's character and account for the world's current state.

Step 3: Expansion of Domain

Local powers tend to become regional and eventually universal as the political communities that carry them expand. The goddess of this spring becomes the goddess of all healing waters; the god of this tribe's sky becomes the sky god; the tribal ancestor becomes the founder of a people. Each expansion increases the deity's abstraction and reduces their connection to specific material features of the landscape.

Step 4: Mythological Consolidation

When literate specialists — priests, poets, scribes — begin recording and systematizing the myths, fluid oral traditions are fixed into canonical forms. Hesiod's Theogony (c. 700 BCE) is the clearest example: it takes a diverse, locally variable set of Greek divine narratives and imposes a systematic genealogical order. After Hesiod, Greek deities have fixed parentage, fixed domains, and fixed relationships — a rigidity that did not exist in the pre-literate tradition.

"Writing is to myth what taxidermy is to an animal. It preserves the form but ends the life. The living myth, told and retold in oral tradition, adapts to its audience and context. The written myth is frozen — authoritative, fixed, and no longer quite alive."

— Paraphrase of Walter Ong, Orality and Literacy (1982)

The Drivers

What Caused the Theistic Turn?

State Formation and Political Theology

The single most powerful driver of the transition from local animism to organized polytheism is the formation of the state. States require legible, universal, transferable religion. A king ruling a territory of many ethnic groups cannot effectively rule through a patchwork of purely local nature spirits — he needs a pantheon that all his subjects can participate in, that can be carried anywhere, and that legitimates his rule through divine appointment.

The Sumerian city-states (from c. 3,500 BCE) are the earliest documented case. Each city had a patron deity (Marduk at Babylon, Inanna at Uruk, Nanna at Ur), and the city-god was understood as the actual owner and ruler of the city — the king was the god's earthly manager. This political theology required a personified, named, mythologically rich deity, not an impersonal local power.

Agricultural Surplus and Temple Economics

The temple economies of the ancient Near East — in which agricultural surplus was collected, stored, and redistributed through temple institutions — required the personification of the deity as an economic actor. The Sumerian temple was literally the household of the god; the god owned the land; the priests were the god's servants managing the estate. This economic model required a deity with legal personhood, the ability to own property, make contracts, and sue and be sued — in other words, a fully personal, named, juridical entity.

Long-Distance Trade and Religious Translation

When communities with different local spirit traditions began trading extensively, they needed to find common religious ground — gods that could be recognized across cultural lines. The process of interpretatio (Greek and Roman practice of identifying their own gods with those of other cultures — Mercury = Hermes = Thoth = Nabu) reflects this drive for religious translatability. Purely local, unnamed spirit-powers cannot be "interpreted" into another culture's framework; named, personified gods can.

The Axial Age (c.c. 800–200 BCE)

The philosopher Karl Jaspers coined the term Axial Age to describe the period roughlyc. 800–200 BCE in which remarkably parallel transformations in religious and philosophical thought occurred independently across Greece, Israel, Persia, India, and China: the emergence of philosophical religion, ethical universalism, the concept of the transcendent, and the critique of polytheism and ritual sacrifice from within the tradition itself.

Plato's critique of the Homeric gods (in the Republic), the Hebrew prophets' insistence on ethical monotheism, the Buddha's agnosticism about the gods, the Upanishadic move toward impersonal Brahman — all represent a second-order religious reflection that pushed further away from the ancient animist substrate. The Axial Age was the great de-materialization of the sacred, the move from power-in-place to power-as-principle.

Source

Karl Jaspers, The Origin and Goal of History (1949) · Karen Armstrong, The Great Transformation: The Beginning of Our Religious Traditions (2006) — accessible treatment of the Axial Age across all four traditions.

What Survived

The Animist Substrate in Historical Polytheism

The transition to theism was never complete. In every historical polytheistic tradition, the older animist, chthonic, and shamanic layers persisted beneath the theological superstructure — visible in folk practice, marginal cults, domestic religion, and the structure of the great festivals themselves.

Greek "Folk Religion"

The distinction between "official" Greek religion (the great Panhellenic festivals, the Olympian pantheon, the major temples) and the actual daily religious life of most Greeks is well documented. Households kept apotropaic (evil-eye averting) amulets at their doors — herm-pillars that are essentially fetish objects. Women consulted local oracles attached to specific springs or chthonic sites. Farmers performed first-fruit rituals before the major Panhellenic calendar had any relevance. The animist layer persisted quietly beneath the theatrical religion of the gods.

Roman Survivals

The Roman state religion of the Olympian-equivalent pantheon (Jupiter, Juno, Minerva, Mars, Venus, etc.) coexisted with a bewildering array of older practices. The Compitalia festival honored the spirits of crossroads — place-spirits without names or mythologies. The Terminalia honored Terminus, the spirit of boundary stones — who resisted being moved from the Capitoline Hill to make room for Jupiter's temple, according to legend. The Robigalia propitiated a divine power of grain rust — not a deity with mythology but simply the power of agricultural blight, addressed to prevent crop destruction.

These Roman indigitamenta — the vast catalog of specialized divine functions — represent the survival of pre-theistic power-thinking within a theistic framework. Each indigitamentum is a name given to a specific, localized, functionally defined power: not a god but a power. The theistic naming is a thin overlay.

Celtic Christianity and the Persistence of Place

When Christianity spread through Celtic Britain and Ireland (5th–7th centuries CE), it did not displace the older sacred geography — it appropriated it. Holy wells (sacred springs) were rededicated to saints. Sacred trees became churchyard yews (many still standing, some among the oldest living organisms in Britain — the Fortingall Yew in Perthshire is estimated at 2,000–5,000 years old). Saint Brigid directly absorbed the functions and festival date (February 1st) of the goddess Brigid/Brigit.

The continuity of sacred place across religious transitions is one of the strongest arguments for the persistence of the animist substrate: the power in the place is recognized regardless of what theological framework names it.

"The church was built on the site of a pagan temple; the temple had been built on the site of a sacred grove; the grove had been identified as sacred because of the spring. The spring was there before all of them, and it is still there, still running. The theological overlays come and go. The water stays."

— Paraphrase of Ronald Hutton, The Pagan Religions of the Ancient British Isles (1991)

Case Study

The Transformation of Demeter: A Worked Example

The cult of Demeter provides perhaps the clearest traceable example of the transition from non-theistic chthonic religion to theistic personification.

The pre-theistic layer: Neolithic and early Bronze Age Aegean religion involved pit offerings to earth-fertility powers — libations poured into the ground, first-fruits buried before harvest, pigs thrown into underground chambers. The power receiving these offerings had no name; it was simply the earth-below, the generative dark from which crops emerge.

The naming: By the Mycenaean period (c.c. 1,450–1,200 BCE), Linear B tablets from Pylos and Knossos mention Da-ma-te — tentatively identified as an early form of Demeter. The name likely derives from da (earth) + mater (mother) — "earth mother." A name has been given to the earth-power, but the entity is still very close to the non-theistic substrate.

The mythological elaboration: By the 7th century BCE, Demeter has a full mythology: daughter (Persephone), an abduction narrative (Hades and Persephone), an emotional story (Demeter's grief causing the first winter), and a mystery cult (the Eleusinian Mysteries) that was the most important religious institution in the ancient Greek world for over a thousand years.

The chthonic survivals: But the Thesmophoria festival — where women threw pigs into underground pits, retrieved them putrefied, mixed the matter with seed grain — persisted alongside the elaborated Demeter cult throughout the entire classical period. The pre-theistic practice and the theistic narrative existed simultaneously, addressing the same underlying chthonic power through two different registers.

Before the GodsXXI. Rituals, Rites & Tenets

Chapter XX

Rituals, Rites & Tenets

Everything preceding has been analysis. This chapter is the practical religion: what was actually done, when, by whom, and according to what principles — assembled as far as the evidence allows into a working account of pre-theistic practice.

No one wrote these down. They are what you would have to assume for the rest to make sense.

On reconstructing principle

A Caution First

There Was No Single Paganism

Before setting anything out, the obvious must be said. There was never one pagan religion with one set of rites and one code. What follows draws together practices attested across thousands of years and thousands of miles, from peoples who had no contact with one another and would not have recognized each other's rites.

The commonalities are real, and they are the reason a chapter like this is possible. But they are structural regularities, not a creed. No community practised all of this, and the tenets in the final section are reconstructions from behaviour rather than anything a pre-Christian people ever wrote down as a list.

With that stated plainly, the patterns are strong enough to be worth assembling.

Daily Practice

The Rites of Ordinary Life

Most religious activity was domestic, frequent, and small. It required no priest, no temple, and no calendar — only the recognition that certain acts should not be performed carelessly.

Daily

The Hearth

Tending, feeding, and never carelessly extinguishing the household fire. A portion of every meal given to the flames before anyone ate. The Roman offering to the Lares and Penates is the formalized version of a practice attested far more widely.

Daily

First Portion

The first of anything — the first drink poured, the first bite, the first of the milking — given away before consumption. Libation to the ground is the most common form, sending the portion downward to the chthonic powers.

On entering

Threshold Acts

Doorways, gates, bridges, and fords marked with apotropaic signs, buried deposits, or spoken formulae. Boundaries are where the ordinary world becomes permeable, and crossing them is done with attention.

On taking

Asking and Thanking

Address to a tree before felling it, to an animal before or after killing it, to a spring before drawing from it. The act is not optional politeness — it converts theft into exchange.

On arriving

Greeting the Place

Acknowledgement of the land-spirits on entering unfamiliar territory. The Icelandic settlers' negotiation with the landvættir before claiming land is the best-documented instance of a widespread practice.

On rising

Reading the Signs

Attention to omens encountered in the ordinary course of the day — birds, weather, dreams, chance words, the behaviour of animals. Divination in its most casual and continuous form.

Ongoing

Feeding the Dead

Food and drink left at graves, in tomb entrances, at household shrines, or poured into the ground. Frequently a fixed share of the household's own meal rather than a special preparation.

As needed

Cleansing

Washing after contact with death, birth, or blood. Water, smoke, fire, or the simple passage of time. Not moral repentance — the restoration of ordinary status after a charged encounter.

Rites of Passage

The Structure of Transition

Arnold van Gennep's The Rites of Passage (1909) identified the three-part structure that life-transition rites share across cultures. Victor Turner later developed the middle phase into a substantial theory of its own.

Phase 1

Separation

The initiate is removed from ordinary life and from their previous status. Physically taken away, stripped of former markers, symbolically killed. The old identity must end before a new one can begin.

Phase 2

Liminality

The threshold state: neither what they were nor what they will become. Ordinary rules are suspended; taboos apply; ordeal, instruction, and revelation occur here. Turner noted that initiates in this phase are typically treated as dangerous and are often secluded.

Phase 3

Incorporation

Return to the community with the new status publicly recognized — new name, new clothing, new marks on the body, new permissions and obligations. The transition is complete only when the community acknowledges it.

The Occasions

Birth and naming. Frequently separated by days or weeks, with the interval treated as dangerous for both mother and child. The Norse ausa vatni — sprinkling with water and conferring a name — marks the child's admission to the household and is attested before Christian influence.

Initiation to adulthood. The most elaborate rite in many societies, and frequently the one attracting ordeal: seclusion, fasting, scarification, tooth removal, circumcision, exposure, or a solitary vigil. The bullroarer's near-universal association with initiation, noted in Chapter VIII, marks these as restricted and dangerous occasions.

Marriage. Typically an exchange between kin groups rather than a union of individuals, and typically governed by the exogamy rules described in Chapter X. The rite commonly involves shared food, joined hands, and transfer between households.

Specialist consecration. The making of a shaman, seer, or ritual specialist, usually involving a near-death experience — illness, isolation, ordeal, or symbolic dismemberment and reassembly. The specialist must have crossed into the other world and returned before they can be trusted to guide others.

Death. The rites described in Chapter V, structured identically: separation of the dead from the living, a liminal interval during which the corpse is dangerous and the mourners polluted, and finally incorporation of the deceased into the community of ancestors.

The Seasonal Round

The Eight Stations and Their Work

The eight-point year set out in Chapters XIV and XV had specific ritual content at each station. What follows is the northern European pattern, which is best attested; other regions divided the year differently.

Midwinter

Vigil and Return

Fires kept through the longest night. Evergreens brought indoors as tokens of persisting life. Feasting on stores. The dead are close. The work is to witness the turn and to survive the interval — the sun is helped back by being attended.

Feb cross-quarter

First Stirring

Ewes come into milk; the first signs of returning light. Purification rites, cleaning of the house and hearth, rekindling of fire. Wells and springs visited. A festival of anticipation rather than abundance.

Spring equinox

Opening the Ground

First ploughing and sowing. Offerings placed in the furrow — grain, food, occasionally figurines. The earth is opened, which is both necessary and a violation requiring acknowledgement.

May cross-quarter

Fire and Pasture

Cattle driven between two fires and moved to summer grazing. Hearth fires extinguished and relit from a communal flame. Greenery cut and brought in. The most exuberant station: the world is fully alive and the community says so.

Midsummer

Peak and Turning

Bonfires on high ground. Medicinal plants gathered at maximum potency. Water sources visited for healing. The sun is at its strongest and begins to fail from this day — the celebration carries an edge.

Aug cross-quarter

First Fruits

The first grain cut and offered before any is eaten. Assemblies, contests, games, and the settling of disputes — the largest gathering of the year in many societies. Debts to the land are paid before the harvest is claimed.

Autumn equinox

Gathering In

Harvest completed and stored. The last sheaf treated with particular care — kept, woven into a figure, or given to the animals. Thanksgiving, and calculation of whether the stores will suffice.

Nov cross-quarter

The Thin Night

Herds culled and meat preserved; the year's account settled. The boundary with the dead is permeable. Places set at table, food left out, fires lit to guide or to ward. Divination is at its most potent. The year ends here.

Occasional Rites

Rites for Particular Needs

Beyond the calendar and the life course, specific circumstances called for specific action.

Before undertaking. Divination preceding any consequential act — war, voyage, settlement, marriage, building. The Roman refusal to act without favourable auspices is the institutionalized form of a widespread reluctance to begin blind.

In sickness. Diagnosis by divination to establish the cause — broken taboo, offended ancestor, hostile working, or soul-loss. Treatment follows the diagnosis: restitution, purification, retrieval, or counter-working. Physical remedy and ritual remedy are not distinguished.

In drought or failure. Escalating offering. When ordinary reciprocity has failed, the value of what is given rises — and the sacrificial evidence in Chapter IX suggests that in extremity this could extend to human life.

On oath. Swearing over a ring, a weapon, a stone, a spring, or with hands in blood or water. The oath's force derives from the object, which is understood to witness and to act. Breaking it damages the oath-breaker directly, without any judging power required.

On killing. Rites for hunters returning with game and for warriors returning from battle. Both have taken life and both are charged; both require cleansing before rejoining ordinary company.

On building. Foundation deposits placed beneath thresholds, hearths, and corner posts — pots, animals, tools, and in some periods human remains. The structure is given something before it is occupied.

The Tenets

Principles Reconstructed from Practice

What follows is not a commandment list. No pre-Christian European people produced one, and the absence is itself significant — a religion without scripture, priesthood, or revelation does not generate codes in that form.

These are instead the working principles that the practices imply: what one would have to believe about the world for this behaviour to make sense. They are inferred, and they are offered as such.

Tenet 1

The world is inhabited

You are not alone among objects. Rivers, stones, animals, trees, and the dead are participants with their own standing. Conduct yourself accordingly, and expect to be observed.

Tenet 2

Nothing is taken freely

Every taking incurs a debt. Give before you take, and give again after. The obligation is not moral improvement but the maintenance of a relationship you depend on.

Tenet 3

The dead remain

Death changes the mode of presence, not the fact of it. The ancestors continue as members of the household and the land. Feed them, name them, and do not forget them — the forgotten dead become dangerous.

Tenet 4

Place is not interchangeable

This spring, this grove, this stone. Power is local and specific, not portable and general. Know the places you live among and what each requires.

Tenet 5

Timing is substance

The right act at the wrong moment is not the right act. Some days are charged and some are ordinary. Attend to the turning of the year, because the turning depends in part on being attended.

Tenet 6

Some things may not be touched

Boundaries exist and contact has consequences — automatic, impersonal, and indifferent to your intentions. Know what is charged, and know how to clean yourself when you have handled it.

Tenet 7

The oath binds itself

A promise sworn on a thing is held by that thing. No judge is needed and no forgiveness is available. This is why oaths were sworn rarely and kept exactly.

Tenet 8

Hospitality is not optional

The guest and the host are bound while under one roof. The stranger may be more than they appear. Across Indo-European traditions this is among the most consistently enforced obligations, with the harshest sanctions for breach.

Tenet 9

Ask before acting

The world can be consulted, and it answers. Do not begin anything consequential blind. Uncertainty is reduced by procedure, and the procedure carries an authority no individual possesses.

Tenet 10

The cycle must be maintained

The world is not going anywhere; it is turning. It does not run by itself, and human ritual participation is part of what keeps it turning. Maintenance, not salvation, is the work.

Tenet 11

Kin extends beyond the living

Your obligations run to ancestors, descendants, and in totemic systems to a species. You are a segment of something continuous, and your conduct is owed to both directions of it.

Tenet 12

Consequence, not judgment

Transgression produces effects the way fire produces burns. There is no ledger, no final accounting, and no forgiveness — only damage, and the procedures for repair. This is the deepest difference from the religions that followed.

No one wrote these down. They are what a person would have had to assume for the rest of the evidence to make sense — recovered from behaviour, not from doctrine, and offered with that limitation attached.

— On reconstructing principle from practice

What Is Absent

The Missing Elements

As instructive as what these traditions contain is what they consistently lack. The absences are structural, not accidental, and they mark the real distance from later religion.

No creed. Nothing had to be believed. Membership was constituted by participation, not assent. There is no pre-Christian European word for heresy, because there was no doctrine to depart from.

No conversion. One did not join. Religion was inherited with place, kin, and language. The concept of persuading an outsider to adopt your rites is largely absent before the missionary religions.

No scripture and no founder. No revealed text, no prophet, no moment of disclosure. Practice was transmitted by doing, and it changed continuously without anyone regarding the change as corruption.

No universal moral law. Obligations ran to specific parties — this kin group, this guest, this place, this ancestor. The idea of duties owed to humanity as such is an Axial Age development, and its absence here is not moral deficiency but a different structure of obligation.

No salvation. Nothing to be saved from and nowhere better to arrive. The dead go where the dead go. The purpose of religious life is the maintenance of working relationships in this world, not passage to another.

Chapter XXII

A Day in the Life

The preceding chapters took the practice apart. This one puts it back together into a single ordinary day — the prayers said at the fire, the words spoken at the door and over the work, the sayings that carried the worldview, and the small precautions of a world understood to be watching back. Where the words survive, they are quoted; where only the shape survives, that is said plainly.

The powers were not visited on holy days. They were addressed at the fire, at the threshold, over the milk-pail, and under the breath.

On the religion of ordinary hours

A Reconstruction

The Texture of an Ordinary Day

Nothing that follows describes a real person. It describes a composite — a day assembled from practices attested across many peoples and many centuries, arranged into a single waking-to-sleeping sequence so that the shape of lived religion becomes visible. As Chapter XXI insisted, no community did all of this, and the arrangement is ours, not theirs.

But the material is not invented. The words quoted below are real and sourced: a Roman farmer's prayer copied out by Cato in the second century BCE, an Anglo-Saxon field-blessing preserved in an eleventh-century manuscript, a German horse-charm written down by a monk, a stanza of Norse gnomic verse, a catalogue of Athenian anxieties set down by a pupil of Aristotle. What we cannot recover is the tone — whether these acts were performed in fear, in comfort, or in the half-attention with which anyone does a familiar thing. The words survive. The feeling behind them mostly does not.

First Hour

Before Light: The Hearth

The fire is never fully out. It is banked at night and woken in the morning, and in a great many traditions the one act that must never happen is its careless extinction — to let the household fire die was to let something die. The day therefore begins where religion was most concentrated and least formal: at the hearth, which was altar, calendar, and the seat of the dead in a single object.

The first words of the day were spoken here, and the first portion of anything was given here before any person consumed it. In Rome this was the daily offering to the Lares and Penates — the household powers and the guardians of the store — a little food set on the hearth or cast into the flames at the main meal, and a fuller offering on the Kalends, Nones, and Ides. Cato, writing a working manual for estate owners, preserves the register exactly: the farmer addresses Janus and Jupiter with wine before the harvest offering, in words that are less worship than negotiation — bonas preces precor, "I pray good prayers, that thou be willing and favourable." The formula is the same one used at the fire in the morning and at the field at noon: name the power, state the gift, ask the return. This is the reciprocity the book has called do ut des, "I give that you may give," in its smallest daily form.

On waking

The First Words

A greeting or short address to the hearth-power and the household dead before the day's speech began. Rarely recorded, because it was too ordinary to write down — we infer it from the formalized versions that were.

Before eating

The First Portion

The first of the bread, the first of the drink, the first of the milking, given to the fire or poured to the ground before anyone ate. The Roman flouring of the sacrificial victim with salted meal — mola salsa — is the temple version of the pinch of salted flour cast into the domestic flame.

Threshold

The Door and the Road: Crossing Out

To leave the house was to cross a boundary, and boundaries were where the ordinary world became permeable. The doorway had its own powers — the Romans, with a thoroughness that later Christians found absurd, assigned separate spirits to the leaf of the door (Forculus), the threshold (Limentinus), and the hinge (Cardea); Augustine ridicules the list in The City of God, which is precisely how we know it existed. One did not step on the threshold but over it; a bride was carried across it; foundation deposits lay beneath it. In the Greek house the same function was carried by the herm at the door and by Hekate, whose little offerings were set out where the road met the gate.

Once outside, the day was read as much as lived. Every ordinary thing could be a sign (Chapter XI): the flight of a bird to the left or right, a chance word overheard at the crucial moment — the Greek klêdôn, an omen taken from a stranger's unwitting speech — the behaviour of animals, the first person met. Theophrastus, cataloguing the Athenian who took all of this too far, describes a man who will not continue down a road if a weasel has crossed it until someone else has passed or he has thrown three stones over the way. That is the excess; the ordinary form was simply attention. And crossing into the tongue's own danger, the safest speech near anything sacred was none at all: the Roman herald's call favete linguis — "favour it with your tongues," keep an auspicious silence — and its Greek twin euphēmeîte both mean, at bottom, say nothing ill-omened now.

The Day's Work

Asking Before Taking

The largest part of daily religious speech was not addressed to gods at shrines. It was addressed to the world one was about to use. A tree was spoken to before it was felled, a spring before water was drawn, an animal before or after it was killed. The act converts theft into exchange, and its logic is fully exposed in one of the most revealing texts to survive from Roman practice — Cato's instruction for thinning a sacred grove. Before cutting, the farmer sacrifices a pig and says:

Primary Source — Cato, De Agricultura 139

Si deus, si dea es, quoium illud sacrum est… — "Whether thou be a god, or whether thou be a goddess, to whom this grove is sacred, as it is right to make thee a sacrifice of a pig for the thinning of this sacred grove, and to this intent, whether I do it or another at my bidding, may it rightly be done."

The power is real, present, and owed acknowledgement — and its name and even its sex are unknown to the man addressing it. Si deus, si dea, "whether god or goddess," is the exact verbal fingerprint of the pre-theistic worldview this book has traced: a power of the place, encountered rather than named, approached with a formula built to cover an identity nobody could be sure of. The same inclusive hedging runs through Roman public prayer ("or by whatever name thou wishest to be called").

The field itself was addressed. The Anglo-Saxon Æcerbot, or "field-remedy" — recorded around the eleventh century, and layered with Christian mass and pre-Christian invocation in the same breath — prescribes a full morning ritual for a barren field: sods cut at night and dressed with oil, honey, milk, and herbs; the ploughman turning sunwise; the plough-beam anointed with incense and salt and fennel. As the seed goes onto the plough, he says:

Primary Source — The Æcerbot Charm

Erce, Erce, Erce, eorþan modor — "Erce, Erce, Erce, mother of earth…"
Hal wes þu, folde, fira modor — "Hale be thou, Earth, mother of men."

The plants of the healer's and cook's world were addressed the same way. The Old English Nine Herbs Charm speaks directly to each herb as a person with a history — Gemyne ðu, mucgwyrt, hwæt þu ameldodest, "Remember, Mugwort, what you made known" — before it is gathered and used. To take from the living world was to enter a relationship with it, and the relationship was conducted in words.

Working Words

Words That Do Things: Charm and Cure

Between prayer and medicine there was no border. When a beast went lame, a wound would not close, or a fever held, the remedy was physical and verbal at once (Chapter XXI, "In sickness"). The spoken part had its own names — the Latin carmen (from which "charm"), the Norse galdr, the Old English gealdor — and it worked by naming the damage and commanding it into order. The clearest surviving example is the second of the two Merseburg Charms, written down in Old High German but plainly older, for a horse that has wrenched its foot. After a scene of gods riding in the wood and working the cure, it ends in the operative words:

Primary Source — The Second Merseburg Charm

ben zi bena, bluot zi bluoda, lid zi geliden, sose gelimida sin — "bone to bone, blood to blood, limb to limb, as though they were glued."

That formula is not a local invention. "Bone to bone, sinew to sinew, blood to blood" appears in an Indian healing hymn of the Atharvaveda more than two thousand years earlier, and again in Scottish and Orcadian bone-setting charms recorded in the nineteenth century. The persistence is the point: the everyday words a person used to mend a sprain could be older than any god they named, transmitted within families, said low and exactly because their power was thought to lie in getting them right. Saliva did similar work — Pliny records the reflex of spitting on a wound, into the bosom, or three times to turn away a bad encounter, a gesture the Greek world shared. These were the reflexes of a hand that expected the world to respond.

What People Said

The Common Tongue: Greetings, Blessings, Sayings

Much of the religion lived in ordinary speech, in phrases whose religious content had gone quiet with use. A Roman greeting wished health (salve, "be well") and a parting wished strength (vale); a serious undertaking opened with quod bonum faustum felix fortunatumque sit — "may it be good, favourable, lucky, and fortunate." A sneeze was an omen and drew a response: Xenophon reports a whole army taking a soldier's sneeze, mid-speech, as a sign from Zeus the Saviour and bowing in answer. To praise a child or a beast was dangerous, because praise drew the envious eye, so the praise was disarmed as it was spoken — praefiscine, roughly "no offence to the powers," or absit invidia, "let envy be absent."

And the compressed wisdom of a people — its proverbs — carried the worldview in portable form. Norse gnomic verse is the richest surviving vein. The most famous stanza of the Hávamál states the whole ethic of a religion without an afterlife of reward in six lines:

Primary Source — Hávamál 76

Deyr fé, deyja frændr, deyr sjálfr it sama; en orðstírr deyr aldregi, hveim er sér góðan getr.
"Cattle die, kinsmen die, and you yourself will die the same; but the fair name never dies of one who earns himself a good one."

The reputation that outlives the person, not a soul that departs to judgment, is what a person builds and leaves behind — and the neighbouring stanzas praise the fire, the sun, and above all the keeping of good faith with a guest, which the book has already identified as among the most fiercely held obligations. At the grave, the everyday formula was gentler and just as worn: sit tibi terra levis, "may the earth lie light upon you," cut so often onto Roman stones that it was abbreviated to four letters.

Reading the Day

A World of Signs: Superstition in Practice

"Superstition" is our word, and it carries a judgement the practices did not carry for those who lived by them; to attend to a permeable world was ordinary prudence, not credulity. Yet the ancients themselves drew a line. Theophrastus sketched The Superstitious Man — the deisidaimōn, the one who fears the powers to excess — as a recognizable and slightly ridiculous type, and in doing so left us the fullest catalogue of everyday belief that survives from the ancient world.

His superstitious Athenian rinses his hands and sprinkles himself at the shrine and carries a bay leaf in his mouth all day; pours oil on the smooth stones at the crossroads and bows; will not tread on a tombstone or go near a corpse; throws three stones across the road when a weasel crosses it; cries "Glory to Athene!" when an owl startles him and, at the sight of a snake, sets up a shrine on the spot; spits into his bosom when he meets a madman or an epileptic; and runs to the dream-interpreters and diviners to ask which god or goddess he should now be praying to. Stripped of its comedy, the list is a straight inventory of the ordinary signs — animals, corpses, boundaries, the mad, the unlucky word — that any person of the period watched for.

The most widespread of all was the fear of the envious eye. A look charged with envy could sicken a child, dry a cow, or blight a crop, and the counter-measure was worn on the body: the Roman fascinum, a phallic amulet hung at doors, on chariots, and around the necks of children (the freeborn boy's bulla served the same purpose), turned the dangerous gaze aside by giving it something absurd to land on. Salt, iron, and running water did related work — salt in every offering, iron laid where a spirit might intrude, the boundary of a stream that many things could not cross. None of this required a god. It required only a world in which force moved between things and had to be managed.

Last Hour

Nightfall: The Dead, the Barred Door, the Banked Fire

As at dawn, the evening's religion centred on the fire and the dead. A share of the meal went to the ancestors at the household shrine or into the ground — frequently a fixed portion of the family's own food rather than anything special (Chapter IV). The dead were near, and nearest at night, and the household's business with them was ongoing rather than occasional.

Ovid preserves the most vivid domestic ghost-rite to survive, performed at the Lemuria in May but built out of gestures that belong to any anxious night. The head of the house rises at midnight, walks barefoot, washes his hands, and throws black beans over his shoulder without looking back, saying nine times, Haec ego mitto; his redimo meque meosque fabis — "These I cast; with these beans I redeem myself and mine." Then he strikes bronze and, nine times more, tells the spirits to leave: Manes exite paterni, "Ghosts of my fathers, go forth." It is exact, it is repetitive, it is done in the dark and not watched — everything the working words of daily religion tended to be.

Then the door was barred with the same attention it was crossed with in the morning; in many traditions fire was never carried out of the house after dark, and never given away on certain nights, lest the luck of the household go out with it. The fire was banked so that it would still be alive at dawn. And the day, which had begun by giving the first portion to the flame, ended by making sure the flame survived the night — the smallest possible image of the whole system: maintenance, not salvation; keeping the relationship in good repair, one ordinary act at a time.

The Limits

What We Can and Cannot Recover

The words in this chapter are weighted toward the places that wrote things down — literate Rome and Greece, and the Christianized early-medieval north, where a monk copying a charm has preserved the last audible echo of something much older. For the long prehistory that most of this book concerns, no spoken word survives at all. The morning greeting to the fire in a Neolithic longhouse, the words said over a Bronze Age grave, the charm muttered over a Palaeolithic wound — these are gone beyond any recovery, and every reconstruction of them is inference from the later analogues and from the mute evidence in the ground.

What can be recovered with confidence is not the wording but the shape: that the powers were addressed at the fire, the door, the work, the meal, and the grave; that the tongue was guarded near anything charged; that the world was read for signs and repaired when disturbed; and that all of this was done in speech, most of it too ordinary ever to be written. The exact prayers of a pre-theistic morning are lost. The fact that there were prayers, and roughly what they did, is not.

Chapter XXIII

Legacy & Modern Relevance

Ancient non-theistic paganism did not end. It continued in folk practice, in seasonal custom, in the felt power of particular places, in the instinctive reverence people bring to ancient trees and wild rivers. And in the twenty-first century, it is being deliberately recovered — for philosophical, ecological, and spiritual reasons that have nothing to do with nostalgia.

The theological overlays come and go. The water stays.

After Ronald Hutton

Survival

The Persistence of Non-Theistic Practice into the Modern Period

Holy Wells and Clootie Trees

Sacred springs have never stopped being sacred in Britain and Ireland. Despite seventeen centuries of Christianity, the practice of visiting holy wells — leaving offerings, seeking healing, tying rags (clooties) to nearby trees — has continued without interruption. There are estimated to be over 3,000 holy wells in Ireland alone, the vast majority of which were pre-Christian sacred sites absorbed into Christian practice by dedicating them to saints.

The clootie tree practice — tying strips of cloth to a tree near a sacred spring, with the belief that as the cloth rots, the illness or suffering it represents rotates away — is attested from at least the medieval period and almost certainly much earlier. The underlying logic is pure animist-chthonic: the spring's power, the tree's growth-and-decay cycle, and the human need for healing are connected in a reciprocal material relationship. No deity is invoked. The power is in the place.

Calendar Survivals

The Christian liturgical calendar is significantly structured by pre-Christian seasonal festivals that proved impossible to displace:

November 1

All Saints / Hallowe'en

Pope Gregory III fixed All Saints Day on November 1st (c. 731 CE), almost certainly to overlay the Celtic Samhain ancestor festival. The pre-Christian content — the dead returning, the boundary thinning — survived as Hallowe'en and remains the dominant cultural form.

December 25

Christmas / Midwinter

The date was selected to overlay midwinter festivals (Roman Saturnalia, Germanic Yule). The Yule log, evergreen decorations, and candle-lighting practices are all pre-Christian midwinter customs that Christian interpretation did not replace but merely renamed.

Spring Equinox

Easter

The very name "Easter" (in Germanic languages) derives from "Ēostre" or "Ostara," an Anglo-Saxon spring festival. The egg and the hare (now the Easter bunny) are pre-Christian spring fertility symbols that the Christian resurrection festival absorbed but did not eliminate.

February 1–2

Candlemas / Imbolc / Groundhog Day

The Celtic Imbolc festival of returning light was overlaid by Candlemas (the Presentation of Jesus) and preserved in folk form as Groundhog Day — the American survival of the European tradition of reading the weather at this cross-quarter day for spring forecast.

Agricultural Folk Practice

European agricultural communities preserved pre-Christian ritual into the 20th century and in some cases to the present. First-fruits offerings (a portion of the harvest given back to the earth), the ritual plowing of the first furrow with ceremony, the "corn dolly" traditions of Britain (the last sheaf of harvest woven into a figure and preserved over winter to ensure next year's crop) — all encode the chthonic reciprocity logic of the ancient agricultural religion.

Ethnographers like Charlotte Burne, Ella Mary Leather, and later Venetia Newall documented these survivals extensively in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, often just before they vanished from active practice. Ronald Hutton's The Stations of the Sun (1996) provides the most thorough scholarly treatment of the British ritual year and the degree to which pre-Christian customs persisted beneath their Christian names.

Modern Revival

The Contemporary Animist Revival

The 20th and 21st centuries have seen a sustained and growing interest in animist and non-theistic pagan religion — not as historical curiosity but as living spiritual practice. Several overlapping movements and intellectual traditions have contributed.

Modern Paganism (Wicca and Beyond)

Gerald Gardner's publication of Witchcraft Today (1954) and The Meaning of Witchcraft (1959) launched the modern Wicca movement. Gardner claimed to have been initiated into a surviving pre-Christian witchcraft tradition; scholars (notably Ronald Hutton in The Triumph of the Moon, 1999) have demonstrated that this claim is almost certainly false — Wicca is a modern invention, heavily influenced by Victorian occultism and the romantic movement.

However, this does not render it intellectually unimportant. Modern Wicca and Neopaganism more broadly — including Druidry, Heathenry, and various forms of reconstructionist paganism — have developed sophisticated theological positions, many of which converge with the scholarly understanding of ancient animism: the immanence of the sacred, the personhood of natural entities, reciprocal ethics, and seasonal ritual observance.

The distinction between "authentic survival" and "conscious reconstruction" matters historically but matters less for the practical and philosophical questions these movements are engaging.

The Deep Ecology Movement

Philosopher Arne Næss coined the term deep ecology in 1973 to describe an ecological philosophy that rejected anthropocentrism in favor of biocentric equality — the view that all living things have inherent value independent of their use to humans. Deep ecology is, in structural terms, a secular philosophical articulation of the animist worldview: the world is populated with beings that matter in their own right, not merely as resources.

Næss and co-thinkers including Bill Devall, George Sessions, and Paul Shepard drew explicitly on indigenous and animist traditions as models for an ecological ethics that did not require a divine creator to ground the value of the natural world.

"The flourishing of human life and cultures is compatible with a substantially smaller human population. The flourishing of non-human life requires such a decrease. Policies must therefore be changed. These policies affect basic economic, technological, and ideological structures."

— Arne Næss & George Sessions, Deep Ecology Platform (1984)

Indigenous Rights and Animist Resurgence

The late 20th and early 21st centuries have seen a major resurgence of indigenous animist traditions globally, driven partly by post-colonial political movements and partly by the ecological crisis making indigenous land-management knowledge newly relevant. The recognition (in New Zealand, India, Ecuador, and elsewhere) of legal personhood for rivers, forests, and mountains — drawing on Māori, Andean, and other indigenous frameworks — is an animist political claim translated into legal form: the river is a person with rights, not a resource.

The 2017 recognition of the Whanganui River in New Zealand as a legal person with rights equivalent to a human being — Te Awa Tupua, "the river as an indivisible and living whole" — is one of the most significant legal events in the history of animist thought. It translates a Māori animist worldview directly into the framework of modern law.

Philosophical Significance

What Ancient Non-Theistic Paganism Offers Today

An Ecological Ethics Without a Creator

One of the most important philosophical resources in ancient animism is its capacity to ground an ecological ethics without reference to a divine creator. In the Abrahamic traditions, the ethical relationship with nature is typically grounded in stewardship theology: humans are appointed as caretakers of God's creation. This grounds care for nature in human obligation to a divine authority.

The animist alternative grounds care for nature in the personhood of natural entities themselves. You do not destroy the river because the river is a person — a subject with its own mode of being, a participant in the community of life — not because a god commanded you to care for it. This is a philosophically stronger position for secular environmental ethics, and it does not require the theological framework it emerged from.

Immanence and the Re-Enchantment of the World

Philosopher Charles Taylor, in A Secular Age (2007), described modernity as characterized by "disenchantment" — the gradual stripping of meaning, agency, and sacrality from the natural world, leaving a flat universe of brute matter and physical law. Max Weber had made the same observation earlier (Science as a Vocation, 1917): the price of scientific rationalization was the loss of the sense that the world is inhabited by powers that matter.

Ancient animism offers what Taylor calls a "re-enchantment" — not by returning to prescientific cosmology but by recovering the experiential and relational orientation that preceded it. The world is not more meaningful because a god designed it. It is meaningful because it is real — because the spring actually flows, the tree actually grows, the ancestor's life actually mattered — and relationship with these realities is the primary mode of human existence.

Death and the Community of the Living and Dead

Ancestor religion offers a model for relating to death that neither denies it (secular materialism) nor transcends it (afterlife theology) but integrates it. The dead remain part of the community — changed, but present; unable to speak in the usual way, but able to be addressed, honored, and learned from.

This model of continued membership for the dead — what anthropologist Thomas Laqueur in The Work of the Dead (2015) traces through all human funeral practice — resonates with contemporary grief psychology's understanding of continuing bonds theory: the idea that healthy mourning involves maintaining a continuing relationship with the dead, not breaking the bond and "moving on."

Reciprocity as Ecological and Social Ethics

The do ut des principle — give so that you may receive — is not naive transactionalism. It is a model of relationship that acknowledges dependence, demands gratitude, and constrains extraction. You do not take from a river without acknowledging the taking and offering something in return. You do not clear a forest without recognizing what you have received and what you owe.

Robin Wall Kimmerer's Braiding Sweetgrass (2013) articulates this indigenous animist ethic most clearly for a contemporary audience: the Potawatomi language has a grammar that treats plants and animals as animate, as grammatical subjects — not "it" but "ki" or "they." Language shapes perception; a language that treats the world as person-populated is a language that makes the animist ethic available at the level of thought itself.

"What if you were suddenly hearing [a language] in which the beingness of asters and goldenrods was noun-like, in which action words were the fundamental way of knowing the world? . . . In English, we never have to think about the animacy of a cedar tree. But in Potawatomi . . . a cedar tree is not an it but a living being."

— Robin Wall Kimmerer, Braiding Sweetgrass (2013)

Conclusion

What These Traditions Were, and What They Remain

Ancient non-theistic paganism was not a failed attempt at theology. It was a coherent, sophisticated, and durable orientation to existence that served human communities for at least 40,000 years — longer than any subsequent religious tradition by a factor of many. Its commitments were not beliefs to be defended but practices to be performed: gifts offered, boundaries respected, ancestors honored, seasons marked, places tended.

It did not require a creator, a revelation, a scripture, a clergy, or a universal claim to truth. It required attention — to the specific spring, the specific ancestral dead, the specific season, the specific animal. This localism, which appeared to Enlightenment thinkers as narrowness, is now recognizable as one of its deepest strengths: it generated care for the specific and present, not for an abstraction.

The world it described — alive, inhabited, reciprocal, local, cyclical — is not a prescientific error that modernity has corrected. It is a mode of perceptual and ethical engagement with a world that is, in fact, alive in the ways that matter most: it can be damaged, it can recover, it requires attention and respect, and it will outlast any particular human community's framework for describing it.

The gods came later. The springs and the ancestors and the animals and the cycles were always there. And they still are.
